Methodology

Every figure on this site is copied from an official source — the IRS, the Social Security Administration, or the state agency that sets the rate — and every page records the date it was checked. This page shows the arithmetic, names the sources, and states plainly what our calculators do not model.

The calculation, step by step

A paycheck calculator is not a mystery box. It is a short sequence of subtractions in a fixed order, and the order matters, because some are taken from your gross pay and some from a smaller, already-reduced number. Here is the sequence we use for every state.

Step 1 — Start from annual gross pay

Everything begins with gross wages for the year, before anything is withheld. If you are paid hourly, that is your hourly rate multiplied by the hours you actually work in a year, not a notional 2,080.

Step 2 — Subtract the federal standard deduction

Federal income tax is charged on taxable income, not gross income. For the 2026 tax year the standard deduction is $16,100 for a single filer, $32,200 for a married couple filing jointly, and $24,150 for a head of household. We subtract it first. If the result would be below zero, taxable income is zero, never negative.

Step 3 — Apply the federal brackets one band at a time

This is where most mental estimates go wrong. A marginal rate applies only to the dollars inside its band, not to your whole income. Someone in the 22 percent bracket does not pay 22 percent of their salary. They pay 10 percent on the first slice, 12 percent on the next, and 22 percent only on what is left above the threshold. We walk the bands in order and add up the tax owed in each.

Step 4 — Add Social Security, up to the wage base

Social Security is withheld at 6.2 percent of gross wages, not of taxable income, and it stops once you have earned $184,500 in 2026. Above that ceiling, nothing more comes out for Social Security for the rest of the year. Your employer pays a matching 6.2 percent, which never appears on your pay stub and is not part of your take-home calculation.

Step 5 — Add Medicare, which has no ceiling

Medicare is 1.45 percent of all gross wages, with no wage base at all. Above $200,000 in wages, an Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9 percent applies to the excess. That surtax is on the employee only. Employers do not match it.

Step 6 — Add whatever the state takes

This is the part that differs by state, and it is the reason this site exists. Some states levy a progressive income tax with their own brackets and their own deductions. Some levy a flat rate. Nine levy no tax on wage income at all — but several of those still withhold payroll contributions that people forget about, which is exactly why no income tax does not mean nothing comes out. Washington is the clearest example: no income tax, yet Paid Family and Medical Leave and WA Cares are both withheld from every check.

One state also changes what Step 1 means. Most states start, as the federal government does, from your pay after a 401(k) contribution. Pennsylvania does not: it treats an elective deferral as compensation at the moment it is made, so its 3.07 percent applies to your gross pay whether you save for retirement or not. Our engine carries a per-state flag for this rather than assuming the federal treatment everywhere, because the difference is invisible in the result and would otherwise understate the tax for every Pennsylvanian who contributes. Source, read on August 28, 2026: the Pennsylvania Personal Income Tax Guide, Gross Compensation, which states that such contributions “are not excludable from the employee’s Pennsylvania income”.

State figures

State rates come from the agency that actually sets them, not from a secondary summary, and they are listed on each state’s own page rather than here. For Washington that means the Employment Security Department for the Paid Family and Medical Leave premium (1.13 percent in total for 2026, of which the employee pays 71.43 percent) and the WA Cares Fund for long-term care (0.58 percent of gross wages, with no wage cap). Both were read on August 22, 2026. See the Washington paycheck calculator for how they land on a real salary.

For Pennsylvania it means the Department of Revenue for the flat income tax rate — 3.07 percent, taken from the state’s own 2026 form REV-413 (I), whose worksheet reads “Multiply Line 1 by 3.07 percent (0.0307)” — and the Department of Labor & Industry for the employee unemployment contribution (0.07 percent of gross wages, with no wage cap, in force for “2023 and thereafter”). Both were read on August 28, 2026. See the Pennsylvania paycheck calculator for what they come to on a real salary, and for the local earned income tax we deliberately do not model.

For Michigan it means the Department of Treasury for both figures that matter: the flat 4.25 percent rate and the $5,900 personal exemption, read on the state’s own form 446, the 2026 Michigan Income Tax Withholding Guide, whose header line states them together. The Detroit rates named on the state page — 2.4 percent resident and 1.2 percent non-resident — come from the same department’s form 5469, the 2026 City of Detroit Income Tax Withholding Guide. Both were read on August 29, 2026.

One honest note about how those two documents were read. www.michigan.gov refuses automated requests: it answers HTTP 403 to a plain fetch, to a browser user-agent, and to a real headless browser alike, and the Michigan Legislature’s site does the same. The two PDFs were therefore read in the copies preserved by the Internet Archive — the State of Michigan’s own files, byte for byte, at a dated snapshot, rather than any third party’s summary of them. We would rather tell you that than quote a figure without saying where we got it. See the Michigan paycheck calculator for what the rate comes to on a real salary, and for the city income tax we deliberately do not model.

For Utah it means two sources rather than one, because the first was out of date. The rate is 4.45 percent for 2026, cut from 4.5 percent, and we took it from the statute itself: Utah Code section 59-10-104 sets the tax at state taxable income multiplied by “4.45%”, marked “Amended by Chapter 250, 2026 General Session”. The Utah State Tax Commission’s Publication 14, Withholding Tax Guide, revision 4/26 and effective for pay periods beginning on or after June 1, 2026, agrees on every one of its eight schedules and supplies the rest: Utah grants no deduction and no exemption, only a taxpayer credit of $485 a year for a single filer and $970 filing jointly, reduced by 1.3 percent of wages above $9,348 or $18,696 and never taken below zero. Both were read on September 2, 2026.

Two honest notes about Utah. First, the Tax Commission’s own income tax rate page still read “January 1, 2025 – current: 4.5% or .045” on the day we wrote this. It had not caught up with the 2026 cut, and a calculator built on that one page would be wrong all year. That is why we read the statute as well — a single agency page is not a source we will publish a number on. Second, the official link to Publication 14 answered HTTP 404 on the state’s own server, including from the page that publishes it, so the guide was read in the Internet Archive’s dated copy of the Tax Commission’s own file, the same detour we had to take for Michigan. See the Utah paycheck calculator for what the credit is worth at each salary, and where it runs out.

For Ohio it means the statute, because the Department does not publish 2026 anywhere on its rate page. Ohio Revised Code section 5747.02 gives the whole schedule in one sentence: no tax at or below $26,050 of taxable income, and above it, for taxable years beginning in 2026 and thereafter, “$332.00 plus 2.75% of the amount in excess of $26,050”. Section 5747.025 and the Department’s 2025 Ohio IT 1040 instruction booklet give the personal exemption, which is claimed per person and whose amount falls as income rises: $2,400 up to $40,000 of income, $2,150 to $80,000, $1,900 above — and, for 2026, nothing at all above $500,000, a ceiling the legislature lowered from $750,000. All were read on September 2, 2026.

Three honest notes about Ohio, because it was the hardest state here to source. First, tax.ohio.gov’s own annual rates page answers normally and then says, in its opening line, that it lists the brackets “for 2005 through 2025”. There is no 2026 on it. Second, codes.ohio.gov refuses connections from here entirely, so the statute was read in the Internet Archive’s dated copy, while the booklet and the 2026 withholding tables came from dam.assets.ohio.gov, the state’s own asset server, which answers when the main site does not. Third, the exemption is indexed to inflation each August and Ohio had not published the 2026 figure when we read it; we use the 2025 amounts because the statute forbids the adjustment from ever producing a lower number, so they are a floor rather than a guess. The most this can cost you is one $50 indexation step, which is $1.38 of tax. See the Ohio paycheck calculator for the $332 step this produces, and for the city and school district taxes we deliberately do not model.

Those Ohio city taxes get the same treatment as every other figure here: sourced, or not stated. The Department’s Table LG-11 is the only municipality-by-municipality record it publishes, and the most recent edition covers calendar year 2018 — we checked for later ones and there are none. So we quote from it the rules that do not age (a city rate “cannot exceed one percent without approval by voters”; the tax follows non-residents who work in the municipality; most cities credit tax paid to another one) and, dated as 2018, its numbers: 642 municipalities levying, rates from 0.50% to 3%, a median of 1.5%. We name no individual city’s rate, because some have changed since. Where a figure would have to be guessed, the page says so instead.

What our calculators deliberately do not model

An estimate that pretends to be exact is worse than one that admits its limits. Ours assume a straightforward situation: one job, the standard deduction, wages only. They do not account for:

  • Itemized deductions — mortgage interest, large charitable gifts, state and local taxes above the standard deduction.
  • Tax credits — the Child Tax Credit, the Earned Income Tax Credit, education credits. Credits cut tax owed dollar for dollar and can move the result substantially.
  • The 2025–2028 deductions for tips and overtime. Under the One, Big, Beautiful Bill, qualified tips are deductible up to $25,000 a year, and the FLSA premium half of qualified overtime up to $12,500 ($25,000 filing jointly), both phasing out above $150,000 of modified adjusted gross income ($300,000 jointly). They are claimed on your return, on Schedule 1-A, not through payroll — so they do not change what is withheld from your paycheck, which is what our calculators estimate. If a meaningful share of your pay is tips or FLSA overtime, your final tax bill can be lower than our figure implies. Source: IRS, Working Families Tax Cuts, read August 27, 2026.
  • Pre-tax payroll deductions — 401(k) and 403(b) contributions, HSA and FSA contributions, and most employer health insurance premiums. These reduce taxable income, so a real pay stub often shows less federal tax than our figure.
  • Multiple jobs, or a working spouse’s separate withholding, which routinely causes under-withholding over the course of a year.
  • Non-wage income — self-employment, dividends, capital gains, rental income.
  • Local and city taxes, which exist in a number of states and are set at the municipal level.
  • Your employer’s actual W-4 withholding. Withholding is a running estimate your employer makes; your true liability is settled when you file.

This matters for how you read the result. Our number estimates tax liability on a simple wage situation. It does not predict the exact figure on your next pay stub. Treat a difference of a few percent as normal.

How we keep the numbers current

Federal brackets, the standard deduction and the Social Security wage base are re-set every year, usually announced in the fall for the following tax year. State payroll rates move on their own schedules. Our rate file carries a review date of January 15, 2027, at which point every figure is read again from its official source rather than rolled forward.

We do not copy rates from other calculator sites. Where two official sources exist for the same figure, we check both and publish only when they agree. The 2026 Social Security wage base, for instance, is confirmed independently by the IRS and the Social Security Administration.

Why the numbers are published as a table too

Every calculator page includes a full table of results across a range of salaries, not just an interactive tool. There are two reasons. The first is that plenty of people would rather glance at a number than type one. The second is that an interactive calculator is invisible to search engines and to AI assistants — they read the page, not the JavaScript — so a page with only a tool on it is a page with no answer on it.

Your data

The calculators run entirely in your browser. Salary figures you type are never sent anywhere, and we verified that by watching the network traffic during a real calculation rather than by simply asserting it. There is no account, no email capture and no salary database. See the privacy policy for what our analytics do collect.
