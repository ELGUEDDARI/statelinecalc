Hawaii Paycheck Calculator 2026

Hawaii taxes income across 12 brackets, from 1.40% to 11.00% — the widest range of any state on this site. The top rate does not start until $325,000 of taxable income for a single filer, so almost nobody pays it. On $75,000, a single filer pays $3,983.20 to the state — 5.31% of gross pay, not 11% — and keeps about $57,609 a year.

How this calculator works

Hawaii runs the most detailed income tax schedule of any state published here: 12 brackets where most states now use one or two. That detail is the reason the headline rate misleads so badly. An 11% top rate sounds punishing, but it applies only to taxable income above $325,000 for a single filer, and the eleven bands underneath it do the actual work.

The calculator applies four deductions, in this order:

  • Federal income tax. Your gross pay minus the federal standard deduction for your filing status, run through the 2026 federal brackets. For 2026 that deduction is $16,100 single, $32,200 married filing jointly, $24,150 head of household.
  • Social Security at 6.2% of gross wages, up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500.
  • Medicare at 1.45% of all wages, with no cap, plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on wages above $200,000.
  • Hawaii income tax on your wages less the state standard deduction of $8,000 for a single filer, run through the 12 brackets below.

Rates come from the agencies and the statutes that set them: the IRS for the federal brackets, the standard deduction and FICA, cross-checked against the Social Security Administration for the wage base. The Hawaii figures were read on September 5, 2026 in the law itself — Act 46, Session Laws of Hawaii 2024, obtained from the State Capitol's own document server — rather than in any summary of it. Our full sourcing is on the methodology page.

Two traps worth naming, because they are easy to fall into. Act 46 contains two bracket tables: one for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2024, which covers 2025 and 2026, and another for years beginning after December 31, 2026. Reading the second one would put every figure on this page a year ahead of itself. And several summaries still give $4,400 as the 2026 standard deduction; that is the 2024 and 2025 amount, superseded for 2026.

What the calculator deliberately does not do: it does not itemize deductions, model Hawaii's low-income household renters credit or food/excise tax credit, handle multiple jobs, or account for health insurance premiums and other employer benefit deductions. It also does not deduct Temporary Disability Insurance or Prepaid Health Care, for the reason set out below.

What this calculator does not deduct, and why

Hawaii has two payroll withholdings that most state calculators either ignore or guess at. We do neither: we name them and leave them out, because guessing would be worse than omitting.

Temporary Disability Insurance. The Disability Compensation Division's 2026 contribution notice says an employer “may withhold TDI contributions of one-half the premium cost but not more than .5% of the employee's weekly wage, with the maximum not to exceed $7.50”. Two words decide it: may, and one-half the premium cost. Whether anything is withheld, and how much, depends on a policy that cannot be seen from a gross salary. What we can tell you is the ceiling: at most $7.50 a week, $390 a year, on a maximum weekly wage base of $1,500.21.

Prepaid Health Care. The same notice allows an employer to withhold “one-half the PHC premium cost but not to exceed 1.5% of an employee's wages”. Again it depends on the premium, and again the ceiling is what we can state.

This is a genuine difference from Washington, where Paid Family and Medical Leave and WA Cares are compulsory at fixed rates and therefore are modelled here. If your Hawaii employer withholds either of these, subtract it from the take-home figures on this page.

Hawaii hourly paycheck: what your rate is worth after tax

Why we ask your hours instead of assuming 2,080

Most hourly calculators multiply your rate by 2,080 and call it a year. That is 40 hours a week for 52 weeks, which describes a full-time salaried schedule rather than most hourly work. If you are on 32 hours, or 45 with overtime, the assumed figure is wrong before any tax is applied. The calculator above asks for your hours and uses them.

What $20 an hour comes to in Hawaii

At 40 hours a week, $20 an hour is $41,600 a year gross. After federal tax, Social Security, Medicare and Hawaii's income tax, that leaves about $34,094 a year, or $2,841.13 a month. Your real hourly rate — what an hour of work actually puts in your account — is $16.39. Hawaii's share is $1,512.00 for the year, or 3.63% of gross pay.

What $25 and $30 an hour come to

$25 an hour is $52,000 gross and about $41,711 after tax, a real rate of $20.05. $30 an hour is $62,400 gross and about $49,293 after tax, a real rate of $23.70. Hawaii takes $2,251.20 and $3,025.60 respectively — 4.33% and 4.85% of gross. Both are a long way below the 11% headline.

Overtime, tips and shift differentials

Hawaii taxes overtime, tips and shift differentials as ordinary wages. With 12 brackets, extra hours can push part of your pay into the next band, which is why an overtime week is taxed slightly harder at the margin than your base pay — but only the dollars above the threshold move, never the whole salary. The brackets below show exactly where each line falls.

Key facts that affect your take-home pay in Hawaii

The standard deduction nearly doubled for 2026

Act 46 lifted Hawaii's standard deduction from $4,400 to $8,000 for a single filer, from $8,800 to $16,000 for a couple filing jointly, and to $12,000 for head of household. It is written into the statute as a schedule rather than a single change: the deduction rises again in 2028, 2030 and 2031, reaching $12,000 single by then. This is the single largest change to a Hawaii paycheck this year.

12 brackets, and the top one almost never applies

Hawaii's 11% top rate is among the highest state rates in the country and is quoted constantly. For a single filer it begins at $325,000 of taxable income — that is roughly $333,000 of gross salary. A worker on $75,000 is in the 7.60% bracket, four bands below the top.

Your effective Hawaii rate rises smoothly and never reaches the headline

On $25,000 the state takes $431.00, an effective state rate of 1.72%. On $40,000 it takes $1,403.20, or 3.51%. On $75,000 it is 5.31%, and even on $250,000 it is only 7.20%. That gap between 7.20% and the 11% headline is what a progressive schedule actually looks like from inside a paycheck.

Filing jointly doubles every threshold

Unlike states that give couples a fixed extra amount, Hawaii doubles the whole schedule: every one of the 12 bracket thresholds and the standard deduction with them. On $75,000 of household income a couple pays $2,466.40 where a single filer pays $3,983.20 — $1,516.80 a year of difference from filing status alone. Head of household is not an afterthought either: its thresholds are exactly one and a half times the single ones, all the way up.

Your 401(k) contribution does reduce your Hawaii tax

Hawaii starts from federal adjusted gross income, so an elective deferral to a 401(k) or 403(b) is already outside the base. On $75,000 with 6% going in, your Hawaii tax falls by $342.00 a year, on top of the federal saving.

It is worth saying explicitly because the rule is not universal: a Pennsylvania worker making the same contribution on the same salary sees their state tax fall by $0.00 — Pennsylvania treats the deferral as compensation the moment it is made. Hawaii does not.

No county income tax comes out of a Hawaii paycheck

Hawaii is unusually centralised: the state levies the income tax and the four counties do not add one on top. That is a real difference from Michigan, where a Detroit resident pays 2.4% to the city, and from Pennsylvania, where local earned income tax is routine. Hawaii's general excise tax does vary by county, but it is charged on transactions rather than withheld from your pay.

Social Security stops, Medicare does not

Social Security is withheld at 6.2% until your wages reach $184,500 in 2026, then stops for the rest of the year. Medicare has no ceiling at all: 1.45% on every dollar, plus an extra 0.9% on wages above $200,000. This is why take-home pay rises unevenly through the year for high earners — the paycheck after you cross the Social Security wage base is visibly larger.

Common mistakes people make

Applying 11% to the whole salary

This is the big one, and Hawaii's reputation invites it. On $75,000 that arithmetic gives $8,250.00 when the real Hawaii tax is $3,983.20 — an overstatement of $4,266.80 a year. A bracket taxes the dollars above its threshold and nothing else. The 11% band does not open until $325,000 of taxable income.

Using the $4,400 standard deduction

It was correct for 2024 and 2025 and is wrong for 2026. Act 46 raised it to $8,000 single. Using the old figure adds $3,600 to your taxable income and overstates your Hawaii tax accordingly. Several online summaries had not caught up when this page was written.

Reading the wrong bracket table in the statute

Act 46 contains two: one for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2024, and one for years beginning after December 31, 2026. The first covers 2026. The second does not start until 2027, and using it a year early shifts every threshold.

Assuming TDI comes out automatically

It may not. The employer may withhold up to half the premium, capped at $7.50 a week; some employers pay the whole premium and withhold nothing. Budgeting for $390 a year that never leaves your pay is as much of an error as forgetting it entirely.

Expecting the calculator to match the paystub to the dollar

It will not, and no calculator can. Health premiums and benefit deductions come out first, your federal W-4 settings drive the federal share, and a second job changes everything. Treat this as an accurate model of the federal and state layers, and read why your pay stub will differ for the rest.

Example calculation

A single filer earning $75,000 in Hawaii in 2026, with no retirement contribution:

  • Federal taxable income: $75,000 − $16,100 = $58,900
  • Federal income tax: $7,670.00
  • Social Security: $75,000 × 6.2% = $4,650.00
  • Medicare: $75,000 × 1.45% = $1,087.50
  • Hawaii taxable income: $75,000 − $8,000 = $67,000
  • Hawaii income tax across the brackets: $3,983.20 — 5.31% of gross, not 11%
  • Total withheld: $17,390.70
  • Take-home pay: $57,609.30 a year, or $4,800.78 a month
  • Effective tax rate: 23.2%

Not shown: Temporary Disability Insurance, up to $390 a year, and Prepaid Health Care, up to 1.5% of wages. Both are at the employer's discretion and are explained above.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Hawaii income tax rate in 2026?

Hawaii does not have one rate. It has 12 brackets running from 1.40% to 11.00%, which is the widest range of any state published here. For a single filer the top 11% rate does not begin until $325,000 of taxable income. The brackets come from Act 46, Session Laws of Hawaii 2024, in the block that applies to taxable years beginning after December 31, 2024 - which covers both 2025 and 2026.

What is take-home pay on a $75,000 salary in Hawaii?

About $57,609 a year, or $4,800.78 a month, for a single filer with no retirement contribution. That is after $7,670.00 of federal income tax, $5,737.50 of Social Security and Medicare, and $3,983.20 of Hawaii income tax. The effective rate is 23.2%, the highest of the states published on this site.

Did Hawaii's standard deduction change for 2026?

Yes, and it nearly doubled. Act 46 raised it from $4,400 to $8,000 for a single filer, from $8,800 to $16,000 for a couple filing jointly, and to $12,000 for head of household. The statute words it as “for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2025”, which means the 2026 tax year. Several summaries online still quote $4,400; that is the 2024 and 2025 figure.

Why is my effective Hawaii rate so much lower than 11%?

Because a bracket applies only to the dollars above its threshold, never to the whole salary. On $75,000 a single filer pays $3,983.20 to Hawaii, which is 5.31% of gross pay - not 11%. Applying the top rate to the whole salary would give $8,250.00, more than $4,266.80 too much. Even on $250,000 the real state share is 7.20%.

Does Hawaii take Temporary Disability Insurance out of my paycheck?

It may, but this calculator does not assume it. Hawaii's Disability Compensation Division states that an employer “may withhold TDI contributions of one-half the premium cost but not more than .5% of the employee's weekly wage, with the maximum not to exceed $7.50”. The words are may and one-half the premium cost: the real amount depends on a policy we cannot see from a salary. The legal ceiling is $7.50 a week, $390 a year. Prepaid Health Care works the same way, capped at 1.5% of wages. If your employer withholds either, subtract it from the take-home figures here.

What is $25 an hour after taxes in Hawaii?

At 40 hours a week, $25 an hour is $52,000 a year gross and about $41,711 after tax, which is $20.05 an hour in real terms. Hawaii's share is $2,251.20 for the year. At this salary you are in the 7.20% bracket, a long way below the headline 11%.

Does a 401(k) contribution lower my Hawaii tax?

Yes. Hawaii starts from federal adjusted gross income, and an elective deferral to a 401(k) or 403(b) is already out of that figure, so it reduces your state tax as well as your federal tax. On $75,000 with 6% going in, your Hawaii tax falls by $342.00 a year. The rule is not universal: in Pennsylvania the same worker's state tax falls by $0.00 - nothing at all.

How does filing jointly change the Hawaii calculation?

Every bracket threshold doubles and so does the standard deduction, from $8,000 to $16,000. On $75,000 of household income a couple filing jointly pays $2,466.40 to Hawaii where a single filer on the same figure pays $3,983.20 - a difference of $1,516.80 a year. Head of household sits between the two: its thresholds are exactly one and a half times the single ones.

Why might my Hawaii tax differ by a few cents from this figure?

Because the statute rounds and we do not. Act 46 writes each bracket's cumulative starting amount to the whole dollar - $9,600 at 1.40% is $134.40, but the law prints “$134.00 plus 3.20%”. We add the bands up exactly, without rounding along the way. On $50,000 of taxable income that puts us 20 cents above the statutory formula, always in the direction of showing slightly more tax rather than less. It is the only difference between this calculator and Hawaii's own worksheet, and it is deliberate.

Do any Hawaii counties charge their own income tax?

No. Hawaii is unusually centralised: the state levies the income tax and the counties do not add an income tax layer on top. That is a real difference from Michigan, where Detroit adds 2.4%, or Pennsylvania, where local earned income tax is routine. Hawaii's general excise tax does vary by county, but it is charged on transactions, not withheld from your pay.

Compare with other states

The same $75,000 salary, single filer, 2026 rates, state layer only:

  • Illinois — $58,025 take-home, an effective rate of 22.6%. An Illinois worker keeps $415.49 more than a Hawaii worker on the same salary.
  • Utah — $58,255 take-home, an effective rate of 22.3%. A Utah worker keeps $645.70 more than a Hawaii worker on the same salary.
  • Michigan — $58,656 take-home, an effective rate of 21.8%. A Michigan worker keeps $1,046.45 more than a Hawaii worker on the same salary.
  • Nevada — $61,593 take-home, an effective rate of 17.9%. A Nevada worker keeps $3,983.20 more than a Hawaii worker on the same salary.

At $75,000 Hawaii takes more than any other state published here. That ranking is not fixed, though: it comes from the brackets a $75,000 salary reaches, and lower down the scale Hawaii's newly doubled $8,000 deduction and its 1.40% opening band close much of the gap. The states that levy nothing at all remain in a category of their own.
