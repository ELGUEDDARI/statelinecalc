How this calculator works

Michigan has one of the shorter state tax rules in the country: one flat rate, one exemption, no brackets. Your pay minus $5,900 for each exemption you claim, multiplied by 4.25%. That is the whole state income tax. What makes a Michigan paycheck harder to predict than the rule suggests is the layer underneath it — a number of Michigan cities levy their own income tax, and whether one applies to you depends on where you live and where you work rather than on anything about your salary.

The calculator applies four deductions, in this order:

  • Federal income tax. Your gross pay minus the federal standard deduction for your filing status, run through the 2026 federal brackets. For 2026 that deduction is $16,100 single, $32,200 married filing jointly, $24,150 head of household.
  • Social Security at 6.2% of gross wages, up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500.
  • Medicare at 1.45% of all wages, with no cap, plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on wages above $200,000.
  • Michigan income tax at a flat 4.25%, applied after subtracting $5,900 for each exemption. A single filer is modelled with one exemption; married filing jointly with two.

Rates come from the agencies that set them: the IRS for the federal brackets, the standard deduction and FICA, cross-checked against the Social Security Administration for the wage base; and the Michigan Department of Treasury for the state rate and the exemption, read on form 446, the 2026 Michigan Income Tax Withholding Guide, whose header line states both figures. The Detroit rates quoted further down come from the Treasury’s form 5469, the 2026 City of Detroit Income Tax Withholding Guide. Both were read on August 29, 2026. Our full sourcing is on the methodology page.

The one thing this calculator does not model: city income tax. It is the single biggest reason a Michigan pay stub comes in under a statewide estimate, and in Detroit it is a large number rather than a rounding difference. Read the Detroit section below before trusting these figures if you live or work in a city that levies one.

What the calculator deliberately does not do: it does not itemize deductions, model dependants beyond the exemption count implied by your filing status, handle multiple jobs, or account for health insurance premiums and other employer benefit deductions.

Michigan hourly paycheck: what your rate is worth after tax

Why we ask your hours instead of assuming 2,080

Most hourly calculators multiply your rate by 2,080 and call it a year. That is 40 hours a week for 52 weeks, which describes a full-time salaried schedule rather than most hourly work. If you are on 32 hours, or 45 with overtime, the assumed figure is wrong before any tax is applied. The calculator above asks for your hours and uses them.

What $20 an hour comes to in Michigan

At 40 hours a week, $20 an hour is $41,600 a year gross. After federal tax, Social Security, Medicare and Michigan’s 4.25%, that leaves about $34,088 a year, or $2,840.70 a month. Your real hourly rate — what an hour of work actually puts in your account — is $16.39. Michigan’s share of it is $1,517.25 for the year.

What $25 and $30 an hour come to

$25 an hour is $52,000 gross and about $42,003 after tax, a real rate of $20.19. $30 an hour is $62,400 gross and about $49,917 after tax, a real rate of $24.00. The state layer moves very little between them: every extra hour is taxed at the same 4.25%, so almost all of the rise in your effective rate comes from the federal brackets.

Overtime, tips and shift differentials

Michigan taxes overtime, tips and shift differentials at exactly the same 4.25% as your base pay, because there is only one rate and the exemption has already been used up by your regular earnings. The common complaint that “overtime is taxed more” is a federal effect: extra pay is withheld against a higher federal bracket, and nothing about the state layer changes. Enter your usual hours to see the base position, then add the overtime hours.

Key facts that affect your take-home pay in Michigan

One rate, no brackets, for everyone

Michigan applies a single rate of 4.25% to taxable income. There is no bracket structure: a worker on $25,000 and a worker on $250,000 face the same percentage on every dollar above their exemption, and no amount of extra earnings moves you into a higher state rate. The only part of your paycheck that changes shape as you earn more is the federal share.

The $5,900 personal exemption is per person, not per household

Michigan does not have a standard deduction. It has a personal and dependency exemption, and the 2026 value is $5,900 for each exemption you claim on your MI-W4. One for yourself, one for a spouse who is not claiming their own, one for each dependant. Every exemption takes $5,900 out of the reach of the 4.25% rate, which is $250.75 a year in your pocket. A married couple claiming two shelters $11,800, worth $501.50.

This is the figure most often missing when someone works out their own Michigan tax. Multiply your salary by 4.25% and you overstate the tax by $250.75 for every exemption you forgot.

Your effective Michigan rate is always below 4.25%, and rises as you earn more

Because the exemption is a fixed dollar amount rather than a percentage, it shelters a larger share of a small salary than a large one. On $25,000 the state takes $811.75, an effective state rate of 3.25%. On $75,000 it takes $2,936.75, or 3.92%. On $250,000 it is 4.15%. The headline rate is a ceiling the state approaches but never reaches — the opposite of Pennsylvania, where the headline rate and the real rate are the same number at every income.

Your 401(k) contribution does reduce your Michigan tax

Michigan taxable income starts from your federal adjusted gross income, and an elective deferral to a 401(k) or 403(b) is already excluded from that figure. So the contribution cuts your state tax as well as your federal tax. On $75,000 with 6% going in, your Michigan tax falls by $191.25 a year.

It is worth saying explicitly because the rule is not universal, and the exception is close by: a Pennsylvania worker making the same contribution on the same salary sees their state tax fall by $0.00 — Pennsylvania treats the deferral as compensation the moment it is made. Michigan does not.

Detroit takes 2.4%, and it is not in these figures

Detroit levies its own income tax on top of the state’s. Per the Michigan Treasury’s own 2026 withholding guide for the city, the rate is 2.4% for residents and 1.2% for non-residents whose predominant place of employment is Detroit, after an exemption of $600 per person — a much smaller exemption than the state’s.

The scale is easy to underestimate. On a $60,000 salary, a Detroit resident pays $1,425.60 to the city while paying $2,299.25 to the state: the city adds 62% again on top of the state layer. A non-resident working in the city pays $712.80. Nothing on this page models either.

Michigan cities may levy an income tax, and a number do

Detroit is the largest but not the only one. Michigan cities may levy an income tax under the state’s Uniform City Income Tax Ordinance, and and a number do. It is withheld by your employer alongside the state tax, it applies to residents wherever they work and to non-residents on the work they perform in the city, and it is genuinely additional money. Your city’s income tax office is the only reliable source for its rate; a statewide calculator cannot know which city applies to you.

Michigan does not tax your pension the way it taxes your paycheck

Worth knowing if you are close to retirement, though it does not affect a working paycheck. Michigan phases retirement and pension income out of tax by birth year: the Treasury’s 2026 withholding guide sets the subtraction for recipients born 1946 through 1966 at $49,423 single and $98,846 married filing jointly, and a larger allowance again for those born before 1946. Wages are not part of that scheme — everything on this page is the working-age calculation.

Social Security stops, Medicare does not

Social Security is withheld at 6.2% until your wages reach $184,500 in 2026, then stops for the rest of the year. Medicare has no ceiling at all: 1.45% on every dollar, plus an extra 0.9% on wages above $200,000. This is why take-home pay rises unevenly through the year for high earners — the paycheck after you cross the Social Security wage base is visibly larger.

Common mistakes people make

Multiplying the whole salary by 4.25%

The most common error, and it always goes the same way: it overstates your Michigan tax by $250.75 per exemption. The rate applies to your pay after the $5,900 exemption comes off, not before.

Looking for a Michigan standard deduction

There is not one. Michigan gives a personal and dependency exemption instead, and the two are not interchangeable: an exemption is claimed per person, so a family of four shelters $23,600 where a single filer shelters $5,900. Searching for a standard deduction and finding the federal figure leads people to subtract $16,100, which understates their Michigan tax by $433.50.

Using a state figure while living in Detroit

A Detroit resident who budgets on the state rate alone is out by $1,425.60 a year on a $60,000 salary. If a city tax applies to you, add it to everything on this page.

Assuming a city tax only applies where you live

It applies both ways. A Michigan city taxes its residents on all their earnings wherever they work, and non-residents on the work they perform inside the city. Commuting into Detroit from a suburb does not put you outside the city tax; it puts you on the lower non-resident rate.

Expecting the calculator to match the paystub to the dollar

It will not, and no calculator can. Your employer withholds from the MI-W4 you filed, which may claim a different number of exemptions than assumed here; health premiums and benefit deductions come out first; and city tax varies. Treat this as an accurate model of the federal and state layers, and read why your pay stub will differ for the rest.

Example calculation

A single filer earning $75,000 in Michigan in 2026, claiming one exemption, with no retirement contribution and outside a city that levies an income tax:

  • Federal taxable income: $75,000 − $16,100 = $58,900
  • Federal income tax: $7,670.00
  • Social Security: $75,000 × 6.2% = $4,650.00
  • Medicare: $75,000 × 1.45% = $1,087.50
  • Michigan taxable income: $75,000 − $5,900 = $69,100
  • Michigan income tax: $69,100 × 4.25% = $2,936.75
  • Total withheld: $16,344.25
  • Take-home pay: $58,655.75 a year, or $4,887.98 a month
  • Effective tax rate: 21.8%

The same worker living in Detroit pays $1,785.60 more to the city, taking the effective rate to about 24.2%.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Michigan income tax rate in 2026?

A flat 4.25%. Michigan has no brackets: the same rate applies whether you earn $25,000 or $250,000. What it does have is a personal exemption of $5,900 per person for 2026, subtracted from your pay before the rate is applied. Both figures come from the state's own form 446, the 2026 Michigan Income Tax Withholding Guide, whose first line reads “Withholding Rate: 4.25% Personal Exemption Amount: $5,900”.

How much is the Michigan personal exemption worth?

$5,900 of your pay escapes the 4.25% rate, which is $250.75 a year in your pocket for a single filer claiming one exemption. A married couple filing jointly and claiming two exemptions shelters $11,800, worth $501.50. Every dependant you claim on your MI-W4 adds another exemption. This is the figure people most often leave out when they work out their own Michigan tax, and leaving it out overstates the tax by exactly $250.75 per exemption.

What is take-home pay on a $75,000 salary in Michigan?

About $58,656 a year, or $4,887.98 a month, for a single filer with one exemption and no retirement contribution. That is after $7,670 of federal income tax, $5,738 of Social Security and Medicare and $2,936.75 of Michigan income tax — an effective rate of 21.8%. City income tax is not included; a number of Michigan cities levy one, and Detroit's is the largest.

Does a 401(k) contribution lower my Michigan tax?

Yes. Michigan starts from your federal adjusted gross income, and an elective deferral is already out of that figure, so the contribution reduces your state tax as well as your federal tax. On $75,000 with 6% going into a 401(k), your Michigan tax falls by $191.25. This is worth stating because the rule is not universal: in Pennsylvania the same worker's state tax falls by $0.00 — nothing at all.

What is $20 an hour after taxes in Michigan?

At 40 hours a week, $20 an hour is $41,600 a year gross and about $34,088 after tax, which works out at $16.39 an hour in real terms. Michigan takes $1,517.25 of that, because the $5,900 exemption shelters a larger share of a small salary than a large one.

How much is Detroit's city income tax in 2026?

2.4% for residents and 1.2% for non-residents who work in the city, after an exemption of $600 per person. The rates are set out in the Michigan Treasury's form 5469, the 2026 City of Detroit Income Tax Withholding Guide. On a $60,000 salary a Detroit resident pays $1,425.60 to the city on top of $2,299.25 to the state — the city adds 62% again on top of the state layer. This calculator models the state layer only.

Which Michigan cities have an income tax?

A number of them, and Detroit is by far the largest. A Michigan city income tax is levied under the state's Uniform City Income Tax Ordinance and is withheld by your employer alongside the state tax, so it is genuinely additional money. Detroit is the one this page quotes a rate for, because its rate is published in a Treasury withholding guide we can cite. If you live or work in a city that levies one, check your pay stub or your city's own income tax office — no statewide calculator can know which city applies to you.

Why is my Michigan paycheck smaller than this calculator says?

The usual reason is city income tax, which is not modelled here. After that: health insurance premiums and other benefit deductions come out before tax and are not included, your MI-W4 may claim a different number of exemptions than assumed, and a second job pushes your withholding up. The state layer itself is the simplest part of a Michigan paycheck and the least likely to be the surprise.
