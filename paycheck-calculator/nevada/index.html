How this calculator works

Nevada is the simplest paycheck in the country to work out, because the state takes no part in it. There is no state income tax line on a Nevada paystub, and unlike Washington — the other big no-income-tax state in the West — there is no state payroll program deducted from wages either. What is left is federal, and only federal.

The calculator applies three deductions, in this order:

  • Federal income tax. Your gross pay minus the standard deduction for your filing status, run through the 2026 federal brackets. For 2026 the standard deduction is $16,100 for a single filer, $32,200 for married filing jointly, and $24,150 for head of household.
  • Social Security at 6.2% of gross wages, up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500. Earnings above that are not subject to it.
  • Medicare at 1.45% of all wages, with no cap, plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on wages above $200,000.

That is the whole list. Rates come from the agencies that set them: the IRS for the federal brackets, the standard deduction and FICA, cross-checked against the Social Security Administration for the wage base; and the Nevada Department of Taxation for the confirmation that no state income tax exists. Every figure was verified on August 27, 2026. Our full sourcing is on the methodology page.

What the calculator deliberately does not do: it does not itemize deductions, apply tax credits, model multiple jobs, or account for health insurance premiums and other employer benefit deductions. Those are personal to your W-4 and your benefits election, and guessing at them would make the number look precise while being wrong.

Key facts that affect your take-home pay in Nevada

Nevada has no personal income tax, and it is in the state constitution

This is not a rate that happens to sit at zero and could be nudged upward in a budget session. The prohibition is constitutional: Article 10, Section 1 of the Nevada Constitution provides that no income tax shall be levied upon the wages or personal income of natural persons. Changing that takes a constitutional amendment, not an ordinary bill. In practical terms, the zero on your paystub is more durable than a low rate somewhere else.

Note the wording: it protects natural persons. The same article allows taxes on the income or revenue of a business, which is why Nevada can levy the Modified Business Tax described below while your wages stay untouched.

The Modified Business Tax is paid by your employer, not by you

Nevada does levy a payroll tax, the Modified Business Tax, and people sometimes assume it comes out of wages. It does not. The Department of Taxation is explicit that it falls on employers who are subject to Nevada’s unemployment compensation law, charged on total gross wages. The general business rate is 1.17%, with the first $50,000 of quarterly wages exempt. None of it is withheld from an employee.

Unemployment insurance is also employer-only

Nevada’s unemployment insurance contributions are paid entirely by the employer, at 2.95% of wages up to the taxable limit for a new employer, plus a small Career Enhancement Program charge. Several states fund part of their unemployment or disability systems from employee wages. Nevada does not.

No state disability insurance and no paid family leave premium

A handful of states — California, New Jersey, New York, Rhode Island, Washington among them — withhold a percentage of wages for disability or paid family leave. Nevada has neither. This is the single biggest reason a Nevada paycheck edges out a Washington one on the same salary.

Social Security stops, Medicare does not

Once you have earned $184,500 in 2026, Social Security stops coming out for the rest of the year, and your checks get visibly bigger. Medicare never stops, and above $200,000 it gets slightly larger with the 0.9% surtax. High earners in Nevada feel both of these more clearly than elsewhere, because there is no state tax layered on top to blur the effect.

Your effective rate is lower than your bracket

A single filer on $75,000 in Nevada sits in the 22% federal bracket but pays an effective rate of about 17.9% on everything, federal tax and FICA combined. The bracket applies only to the dollars inside it, never to your whole salary.

Tipped work is common in Nevada, and tips are taxable

Casinos, hotels and restaurants employ a large share of the Nevada workforce, so tipped pay is ordinary here rather than exceptional. Tips are wages for tax purposes: reported tips are subject to federal income tax, Social Security and Medicare exactly like your hourly rate. Enter your gross pay including reported tips, and leave out cash tips you have not reported — those are outside what an employer withholds, not outside what you owe.

Common mistakes people make

Assuming no income tax means a huge difference

Moving from a state with a 5% flat tax to Nevada on $75,000 saves you somewhere around $3,000 a year, not a third of your salary. Federal tax and FICA are the same everywhere and make up the overwhelming majority of what comes out. State income tax is the smaller half of the equation almost everywhere.

Forgetting that Nevada makes up the revenue elsewhere

A state with no income tax still has to fund itself. Nevada leans on sales tax and on gaming and tourism revenue. If you are comparing a job offer across state lines, compare the whole cost picture — sales tax, housing, insurance — not just the paycheck line.

Confusing the Modified Business Tax with a wage deduction

It is a tax on the employer measured by payroll, not a deduction from pay. If you see it quoted as a “Nevada payroll tax” and wonder where it went on your paystub, the answer is that it was never there.

Using an annual figure when you are paid hourly

Multiplying an hourly rate by 2,080 assumes exactly 40 hours a week, every week, all year. In shift work with variable hours it usually overstates the annual figure. Enter what you actually expect to earn.

Expecting the calculator to match the paystub to the dollar

Withholding is a running estimate your employer makes from your W-4. Our figure estimates liability on a simple wage situation. A difference of a few percent is normal — see the disclaimer for the full list of reasons.

Frequently asked questions

Does Nevada have a state income tax?

No. The Nevada Department of Taxation states plainly that Nevada residents do not pay state tax on income earned from salaries, wages, or similar compensation, and that the state does not impose a personal income tax at all. Nothing at state level comes out of your paycheck.

What is take-home pay on a $75,000 salary in Nevada?

About $61,593 a year, or roughly $5,133 a month, for a single filer taking the standard deduction in 2026. Total withholding is $13,407.50, an effective rate of 17.9% — and every dollar of it is federal.

Is anything at all withheld from a Nevada paycheck at state level?

No. The Modified Business Tax and unemployment insurance are both employer obligations, not wage deductions, and Nevada has no disability insurance or paid family leave premium. A Nevada paystub shows federal income tax, Social Security and Medicare, and nothing else from the state.

Is take-home pay higher in Nevada than in Washington?

Yes, slightly. Neither state taxes wages, but Washington still withholds Paid Family and Medical Leave and WA Cares. On $75,000 that is about $1,040 a year. Compare it directly on the Washington paycheck calculator.

Why is my Nevada paycheck smaller than the calculator says?

Almost always because of something personal rather than something about Nevada: health insurance premiums, a 401(k) contribution, extra withholding you set on your W-4, or a second job. The calculator models one job, the standard deduction and wages only.

Do I pay Nevada tax if I live in Nevada and work in California?

California will tax the income you earn there, and Nevada has no income tax to credit it against, so you do not get the benefit of Nevada’s rate on that income. This is one of the most common and most expensive misunderstandings in the region. If it applies to you, it is worth an hour with a professional.

Compare with neighboring states

Nevada’s advantage only shows up in comparison. These are the states people actually weigh it against:

  • Washington — also no income tax, but two state payroll programs come out of every check. Nevada is the cleaner of the two.
  • California — the comparison that drives most moves into Nevada, and the largest gap of any pair in the West.
  • Arizona — a low flat state income tax, and a common alternative for the same relocation.
  • Utah — a flat state income tax, with the state line an hour from Las Vegas.
  • Oregon — no sales tax, but a state income tax reaching 9.9%. The mirror image of Nevada.
