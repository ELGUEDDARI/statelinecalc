How this calculator works

North Carolina has one of the shortest state tax rules in the country: one flat rate, one standard deduction, no brackets. Your pay minus $12,750, multiplied by 3.99%. What makes a North Carolina paycheck harder to predict than that rule suggests is not the tax — it is the withholding, which the state deliberately sets a tenth of a point above the tax itself.

The calculator applies four deductions, in this order:

  • Federal income tax. Your gross pay minus the federal standard deduction for your filing status, run through the 2026 federal brackets. For 2026 that deduction is $16,100 single, $32,200 married filing jointly, $24,150 head of household.
  • Social Security at 6.2% of gross wages, up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500.
  • Medicare at 1.45% of all wages, with no cap, plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on wages above $200,000.
  • North Carolina income tax at a flat 3.99%, applied after subtracting the state standard deduction: $12,750 single, $25,500 married filing jointly, $19,125 head of household.

Rates come from the agencies that set them: the IRS for the federal brackets, the standard deduction and FICA, cross-checked against the Social Security Administration for the wage base; and the North Carolina Department of Revenue for the state rate and deduction. The rate was read on NCDOR’s own tax rate schedules and confirmed in form NC-30, the 2026 Income Tax Withholding Tables and Instructions for Employers; the deduction amounts were read on form NC-4, the 2026 Employee’s Withholding Allowance Certificate. Both were read on September 9, 2026. Our full sourcing is on the methodology page.

The one thing to know before comparing this with your pay stub: this page calculates the tax you owe, at 3.99%. Your employer withholds at 4.09%, because the state tells them to. The gap is explained below, and it is the reason a North Carolina stub comes in slightly under a correct estimate rather than over.

What the calculator deliberately does not do: it does not itemize deductions, model the North Carolina child deduction, handle multiple jobs, or account for health insurance premiums and other employer benefit deductions.

North Carolina hourly paycheck: what your rate is worth after tax

Why we ask your hours instead of assuming 2,080

Most hourly calculators multiply your rate by 2,080 and call it a year. That is 40 hours a week for 52 weeks, which describes a full-time salaried schedule rather than most hourly work. If you are on 32 hours, or 45 with overtime, the assumed figure is wrong before any tax is applied. The calculator above asks for your hours and uses them.

What $20 an hour comes to in North Carolina

At 40 hours a week, $20 an hour is $41,600 a year gross. After federal tax, Social Security, Medicare and North Carolina’s 3.99%, that leaves about $34,454 a year, or $2,871.21 a month. Your real hourly rate — what an hour of work actually puts in your account — is $16.56. North Carolina’s share of it is $1,151.12 for the year.

What $25 and $30 an hour come to

$25 an hour is $52,000 gross and about $42,396 after tax, a real rate of $20.38. $30 an hour is $62,400 gross and about $50,337 after tax, a real rate of $24.20. The state layer moves very little between them: every extra hour is taxed at the same 3.99%, so almost all of the rise in your effective rate comes from the federal brackets.

Overtime, tips and shift differentials

North Carolina taxes overtime, tips and shift differentials at exactly the same 3.99% as your base pay, because there is only one rate and the standard deduction has already been used up by your regular earnings. The common complaint that “overtime is taxed more” is a federal effect: extra pay is withheld against a higher federal bracket, and nothing about the state layer changes. Enter your usual hours to see the base position, then add the overtime hours.

Key facts that affect your take-home pay in North Carolina

Your employer withholds 4.09%. You owe 3.99%.

This is the one North Carolina fact that no other state on this site shares, and it is written into the state’s own instructions to employers. Under every worksheet in form NC-30, the 2026 withholding tables, the same sentence appears: “The withholding calculations are based on the individual income tax rate of 3.99% plus 0.1%. This results in a withholding tax rate of 4.09%.”

It is deliberate over-withholding. The state would rather send you a refund than chase a balance, so it instructs employers to take out a tenth of a point more than the law charges. On a $60,000 salary the extra amounts to about $47.25 across the year; on $75,000, about $62.25. It is not a tax and it is not lost — it comes back when you file.

This page models the tax, at 3.99%, because that is what the year actually costs you. If you are reconciling against a pay stub rather than a tax return, add roughly 0.1% of your pay above the standard deduction to the state line, and expect it back later.

One rate, no brackets, for everyone

North Carolina applies a single rate of 3.99% to taxable income. There is no bracket structure: a worker on $25,000 and a worker on $250,000 face the same percentage on every dollar above their standard deduction, and no amount of extra earnings moves you into a higher state rate. The only part of your paycheck that changes shape as you earn more is the federal share.

The rate came down from 4.25%, and 2027 is not settled

North Carolina is in the middle of a legislated series of cuts. The rate was 4.25% for 2025 and is 3.99% for 2026 — on a $75,000 salary that change alone is worth $161.85 a year. What comes next is conditional rather than scheduled: NCDOR’s own wording is that “additional rate changes may apply to tax years beginning with 2027 based on certain rate reduction triggers”, set by Session Law 2023-134. Those triggers are revenue targets. Until they are met, 3.99% is the number, and this page will not quote a 2027 rate it cannot source.

The standard deduction is $12,750, and it is not the federal one

North Carolina sets its own: $12,750 single or married filing separately, $25,500 married filing jointly or surviving spouse, $19,125 head of household. Those amounts are printed on form NC-4. Every dollar of it escapes the 3.99% rate, which is $508.72 a year for a single filer and $1,017.45 for a couple filing jointly.

Two differences from the federal deduction catch people out. There is no extra amount for being 65 or older or blind — the federal deduction has one, North Carolina’s does not. And if you are not eligible for the federal standard deduction at all, your North Carolina standard deduction is zero.

Your effective North Carolina rate is always below 3.99%, and rises as you earn more

Because the deduction is a fixed dollar amount rather than a percentage, it shelters a larger share of a small salary than a large one. On $25,000 the state takes $488.78, an effective state rate of 1.96%. On $75,000 it takes $2,483.78, or 3.31%. On $250,000 it is 3.79%. The headline rate is a ceiling the state approaches but never reaches — the opposite of Pennsylvania, where the headline rate and the real rate are the same number at every income.

Filing jointly moves the deduction, not the rate

A couple filing jointly shelters $25,500 instead of $12,750, exactly twice the single amount, and pays the same 3.99% on the rest. On a $75,000 single income, choosing married filing jointly takes the state tax from $2,483.78 to $1,975.05. There is no marriage penalty in the North Carolina rate itself, because there is only one rate to apply.

Your 401(k) contribution does reduce your North Carolina tax

North Carolina taxable income starts from your federal adjusted gross income — NCDOR says so in as many words — and an elective deferral to a 401(k) or 403(b) is already excluded from that figure. So the contribution cuts your state tax as well as your federal tax. On $75,000 with 6% going in, your North Carolina tax falls by $179.55 a year.

It is worth saying explicitly because the rule is not universal, and the exception is close by: a Pennsylvania worker making the same contribution on the same salary sees their state tax fall by $0.00 — Pennsylvania treats the deferral as compensation the moment it is made. North Carolina does not.

Nothing else comes out at the state level

State income tax is the only state-level line on a North Carolina pay stub. Unemployment insurance is paid by employers: the N.C. Division of Employment Security states that “employers pay unemployment insurance taxes based on employers’ payroll” and that “unemployment taxes are not deducted from employees’ wages”. There is no state disability or paid-family-leave deduction of the kind that takes 1.387% out of a Washington paycheck. If a state with no income tax can still deduct from your pay, a state with one can also stop at that — and North Carolina does.

The child deduction is real, and it is not in these figures

North Carolina allows a deduction for taxpayers who qualify for the federal child tax credit. It is worth an amount per qualifying child that falls as income rises, and it is claimed on your return — or reflected through the allowances you enter on your NC-4. This calculator does not model it, because it depends on facts about your household rather than your salary. If you have qualifying children, your real North Carolina tax is lower than the figure shown here.

Allowances on your NC-4 are worth $2,500 each

The NC-4 works with allowances rather than a simple deduction box. Each one takes $2,500 a year out of the pay your employer applies the withholding rate to — the NC-30 worksheet says to multiply the number of allowances by $2,500.00. Claiming allowances you are not entitled to moves money into your pocket now and out of it at filing; claiming none, when you have children or large itemized deductions, does the reverse.

Social Security stops, Medicare does not

Social Security is withheld at 6.2% until your wages reach $184,500 in 2026, then stops for the rest of the year. Medicare has no ceiling at all: 1.45% on every dollar, plus an extra 0.9% on wages above $200,000. This is why take-home pay rises unevenly through the year for high earners — the paycheck after you cross the Social Security wage base is visibly larger.

Common mistakes people make

Multiplying the whole salary by 3.99%

The most common error, and it always goes the same way: it overstates your North Carolina tax by $508.72 for a single filer. The rate applies to your pay after the $12,750 standard deduction comes off, not before.

Using 4.09% as the tax rate

It is the withholding rate, not the tax rate, and the difference runs the other way from what people assume: 4.09% is what leaves your paycheck, 3.99% is what you owe. Budget on 3.99% and treat the rest as a small forced saving that comes back at filing.

Using the federal standard deduction on the state return

NCDOR flags this one itself: the North Carolina standard deduction and the federal standard deduction are not the same number, and the federal figure does not belong on line 11 of form D-400. Subtracting $16,100 instead of $12,750 understates your North Carolina tax by $133.67.

Expecting an extra deduction at 65

The federal standard deduction grows when you turn 65 or if you are blind. North Carolina’s does not: the amount is the same at 25 and at 75. Retirement income is treated separately from wages, but the deduction itself does not move.

Expecting the calculator to match the paystub to the dollar

It will not, and no calculator can — and in North Carolina there is a structural reason before the usual ones: the 4.09% withholding rate. After that, your employer withholds from the NC-4 you filed, which may claim a different number of allowances than assumed here; health premiums and benefit deductions come out first. Treat this as an accurate model of the federal and state layers, and read why your pay stub will differ for the rest.

Example calculation

A single filer earning $75,000 in North Carolina in 2026, with no retirement contribution:

  • Federal taxable income: $75,000 − $16,100 = $58,900
  • Federal income tax: $7,670.00
  • Social Security: $75,000 × 6.2% = $4,650.00
  • Medicare: $75,000 × 1.45% = $1,087.50
  • North Carolina taxable income: $75,000 − $12,750 = $62,250
  • North Carolina income tax: $62,250 × 3.99% = $2,483.78
  • Total withheld: $15,891.28
  • Take-home pay: $59,108.73 a year, or $4,925.73 a month
  • Effective tax rate: 21.2%

The same worker’s employer will actually hold back $2,546.03 of state tax across the year, at the 4.09% withholding rate — $62.25 more than the $2,483.78 owed. That difference is not an extra cost; it is the refund arriving early.

Frequently asked questions

What is the North Carolina income tax rate in 2026?

A flat 3.99%. North Carolina has no brackets: the same rate applies whether you earn $25,000 or $250,000. It comes down from 4.25% in 2025. The figure is the state's own: the NCDOR tax rate schedules read “For Taxable Years after 2025, the North Carolina individual income tax rate is 3.99% (0.0399)”, and the 2026 NC-30 withholding guide repeats it in its “New for 2026” box. Before the rate applies, you subtract the North Carolina standard deduction — $12,750 for a single filer.

Why does my North Carolina employer withhold 4.09% and not 3.99%?

Because the state tells them to. The 2026 NC-30 withholding tables say, under every worksheet, “The withholding calculations are based on the individual income tax rate of 3.99% plus 0.1%. This results in a withholding tax rate of 4.09%.” The extra tenth of a point is deliberate over-withholding: you pay tax at 3.99% and your employer holds back 4.09%, so the difference comes back as part of your refund. On a $75,000 salary that is about $62.25 a year. This calculator shows the tax you owe at 3.99%, which is why its take-home figure is slightly higher than your pay stub.

What is take-home pay on a $75,000 salary in North Carolina?

About $59,109 a year, or $4,925.73 a month, for a single filer with no retirement contribution. That is after $7,670 of federal income tax, $5,738 of Social Security and Medicare and $2,483.78 of North Carolina income tax — an effective rate of 21.2%. Your employer will actually hold back about $62.25 more than that state figure, at the 4.09% withholding rate, and return it at filing.

How much is the North Carolina standard deduction for 2026?

$12,750 if you are single or married filing separately, $25,500 if you are married filing jointly or a surviving spouse, and $19,125 for head of household. The amounts are printed on Form NC-4, the 2026 employee's withholding allowance certificate. Two things surprise people: there is no extra amount for being 65 or older or blind, unlike the federal deduction, and if you are not eligible for the federal standard deduction your North Carolina one is zero.

Does a 401(k) contribution lower my North Carolina tax?

Yes. North Carolina taxable income starts from your federal adjusted gross income, and an elective deferral is already out of that figure, so the contribution reduces your state tax as well as your federal tax. On $75,000 with 6% going into a 401(k), your North Carolina tax falls by $179.55. The rule is not universal: in Pennsylvania the same worker's state tax falls by $0.00 — nothing at all.

What is $20 an hour after taxes in North Carolina?

At 40 hours a week, $20 an hour is $41,600 a year gross and about $34,454 after tax, which works out at $16.56 an hour in real terms. North Carolina takes $1,151.12 of that, because the $12,750 standard deduction shelters a larger share of a small salary than a large one.

Is unemployment insurance taken out of a North Carolina paycheck?

No. The N.C. Division of Employment Security states it plainly: “Employers pay unemployment insurance taxes based on employers' payroll. Unemployment taxes are not deducted from employees' wages.” North Carolina also has no state disability or paid-leave payroll deduction of the kind Washington workers pay. State income tax is the only state-level line on a North Carolina pay stub.

Will the North Carolina tax rate fall again in 2027?

It may, and nobody can promise it yet. Session Law 2023-134 set the 3.99% rate for 2026 and made every further cut conditional: NCDOR's own wording is that “additional rate changes may apply to tax years beginning with 2027 based on certain rate reduction triggers”, which are revenue targets the state has to hit first. This page models 2026, the year that is settled.

Why is my North Carolina paycheck smaller than this calculator says?

Start with the 4.09% withholding rate above: your employer holds back a tenth of a point more than the tax you owe, on purpose. After that, the usual reasons apply — health insurance premiums and other benefit deductions come out before tax and are not modelled here, the allowances on your NC-4 may differ from the assumption made here, and a second job pushes your federal withholding up.

Compare with other states

The same $75,000 salary, single filer, 2026 rates, state layer only:

  • Georgia — $58,599 take-home, an effective rate of 21.9%. A Georgia worker keeps $510.22 less than a North Carolinian on the same salary.
  • Tennessee — $61,593 take-home, an effective rate of 17.9%. A Tennessee worker keeps $2,483.78 more than a North Carolinian on the same salary.
  • Pennsylvania — $59,238 take-home, an effective rate of 21.0%. A Pennsylvania worker keeps $128.78 more than a North Carolinian on the same salary.
  • Texas — $61,593 take-home, an effective rate of 17.9%. A Texas worker keeps $2,483.78 more than a North Carolinian on the same salary.

North Carolina sits low among the states that tax wages at all: its rate is below Georgia’s and its standard deduction is real, but it cannot reach the states next door that levy nothing. The gap to Tennessee is the whole of the state tax line, and it is smaller than most people expect.
