How this calculator works

Ohio has the lowest state income tax of any state on this site that levies one, and the strangest shape. There are no brackets in the usual sense. Instead the statute says that nothing is owed at or below $26,050 of taxable income, and that above it you owe $332 plus 2.75% of the excess. Taxable income is your pay less a personal exemption for every person in the household — and the size of that exemption itself depends on how much you earn.

The calculator applies four deductions, in this order:

  • Federal income tax. Your gross pay minus the federal standard deduction for your filing status, run through the 2026 federal brackets. For 2026 that deduction is $16,100 single, $32,200 married filing jointly, $24,150 head of household.
  • Social Security at 6.2% of gross wages, up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500.
  • Medicare at 1.45% of all wages, with no cap, plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on wages above $200,000.
  • Ohio income tax: your pay less $2,400, $2,150 or $1,900 per exemption depending on income; then nothing on the first $26,050 of what is left, and $332 plus 2.75% above it.

Rates come from the agencies that set them: the IRS for the federal brackets, the standard deduction and FICA, cross-checked against the Social Security Administration for the wage base. The Ohio figures come from the statute and the Department’s own booklet, because the Department’s rate page does not publish 2026 at all. Ohio Revised Code section 5747.02 supplies the threshold and the rate; section 5747.025 and the 2025 Ohio IT 1040 instruction booklet supply the exemption amounts. Both were read on September 2, 2026. Our full sourcing is on the methodology page.

Two things this calculator does not model, and in Ohio they matter more than anywhere else on this site. Ohio cities levy their own municipal income tax, collected by the city rather than the state: 242 Ohio cities and 400 villages levied one. Ohio school districts may levy one too, on a separate return. Read both sections below before treating these figures as your whole tax bill.

What the calculator deliberately does not do: it does not itemize deductions, model dependants beyond the exemption count implied by your filing status, handle Ohio’s separate 3% rate on business income, handle multiple jobs, or account for health insurance premiums and other employer benefit deductions.

Ohio hourly paycheck: what your rate is worth after tax

Why we ask your hours instead of assuming 2,080

Most hourly calculators multiply your rate by 2,080 and call it a year. That is 40 hours a week for 52 weeks, which describes a full-time salaried schedule rather than most hourly work. If you are on 32 hours, or 45 with overtime, the assumed figure is wrong before any tax is applied. The calculator above asks for your hours and uses them.

What $20 an hour comes to in Ohio

At 40 hours a week, $20 an hour is $41,600 a year gross. After federal tax, Social Security, Medicare and Ohio’s share, that leaves about $34,905 a year, or $2,908.76 a month. Your real hourly rate — what an hour of work actually puts in your account — is $16.78. Ohio takes only $700.50 of that for the whole year, which is 1.68% of gross. Federal tax and FICA take many times more.

What $25 and $30 an hour come to

$25 an hour is $52,000 gross and about $42,976 after tax, a real rate of $20.66. $30 an hour is $62,400 gross and about $51,046 after tax, a real rate of $24.54. The whole state layer moves from $986.50 to $1,272.50 across that gap — less than a single week of the pay rise itself. In Ohio the state is rarely the reason a raise disappoints.

Working a few hours a week? Ohio may take nothing at all

A single filer does not owe the state anything until about $28,450 a year, which at 40 hours a week is $13.68 an hour. Below that the exemption and the $26,050 threshold together cover the whole wage. Federal tax and FICA still apply — FICA from the first dollar — so a small paycheck is never untaxed. It is only untaxed by Ohio.

Overtime, tips and shift differentials

Ohio taxes overtime, tips and shift differentials at the same 2.75% as base pay. There is one case where extra hours cost far more than 2.75%, and it is the $332 step: if your annual pay lands just under $28,450, the overtime that pushes you past it brings the whole $332 with it. Above that point the step is behind you and never reappears.

Key facts that affect your take-home pay in Ohio

Ohio no longer has five brackets, and the top rate is not 4%

This is the single most common piece of stale information about Ohio pay. The state used to run a multi-bracket schedule topping out near 4%; it has been collapsed year after year. For 2026 and thereafter the statute reads, in full, “$332.00 plus 2.75% of the amount in excess of $26,050”. One rate, one threshold. If a calculator shows you an Ohio rate near 4%, or a table of five brackets, it has not been updated.

The first $26,050 of taxable income is not taxed — but the next dollar costs $332.03

Read the statute closely and there is a step in it, not a slope. Nothing is owed at or below $26,050 of taxable income. Above it, you owe $332 plus 2.75% of the excess. So a single filer on $28,450 of salary owes Ohio $0.00, and the same filer on $28,451 owes $332.03. One dollar of pay, $332.03 of tax.

This is not our reading of an ambiguity. Ohio’s own IT 1040 booklet prints a bracket row reading “0.000%” up to $26,050 and then, in its worked example, says the taxpayer “owes $342 on the first $26,050 of income” — the 2025 figure, which becomes $332 for 2026. The same structure appears in the statute for 2024 and 2025 as well. A married couple filing jointly reaches the step later, at about $30,850 of salary, because they claim two exemptions.

The personal exemption shrinks as you earn more

Ohio has no standard deduction. It has a personal and dependency exemption claimed for yourself, your spouse and each dependant — and unlike every other state on this site, the amount depends on your income:

  • $2,400 per person if your income is $40,000 or less;
  • $2,150 per person between $40,001 and $80,000;
  • $1,900 per person above $80,000;
  • and for 2026, nothing at all above $500,000 of income — a threshold the legislature lowered from $750,000 for 2026.

Each tier boundary is a small step of its own: crossing $40,000 costs about $6.88 of extra tax, and crossing $80,000 about $6.88. Small, but they are why your Ohio tax rises slightly faster than 2.75% of each extra dollar.

Your effective Ohio rate never reaches 2.75%

Because the first $26,050 escapes and the exemption comes off first, the share Ohio actually takes stays well under the headline rate at every salary. On $30,000 the state takes $374.63, an effective state rate of 1.25%. On $75,000 it is 2.16%. Even on $250,000 it is only 2.58%. The headline rate is a ceiling Ohio approaches and never reaches.

Your employer withholds at 3.4%, not 2.75%

The Department’s withholding tables effective August 1, 2026 instruct employers, verbatim: “If the wages exceed $1,923, use the last row of the table plus 3.400% of the excess over $1,923.” That is a withholding rate well above the 2.75% tax. Withholding is designed to overshoot so that nobody owes at filing, and in Ohio it overshoots by a wide margin.

The practical effect: your pay stub will show more Ohio tax than this page says you owe, and the difference comes back as a refund. On $75,000 the state tax we calculate is $1,619.00; a full year withheld at 3.4% would be roughly $2,550. We show what you owe, which is what your after-tax income actually is over a full year.

Your 401(k) contribution does reduce your Ohio tax

Ohio taxable income starts from your federal adjusted gross income, so an elective deferral to a 401(k) or 403(b) is already excluded. On $75,000 with 6% going in, your Ohio tax falls by $123.75 a year. That is a small sum, and it is small for the right reason: the rate itself is small. The same contribution is worth many times more against your federal tax.

It is worth stating because the rule is not universal: a Pennsylvania worker making the same contribution sees their state tax fall by $0.00 — nothing at all.

Your city can easily take more than the state does

This is the fact that matters most on this page, and it is the one usually left out. 642 Ohio municipalities — 242 cities and 400 villages — levied their own income tax, according to the Department of Taxation’s own Table LG-11 for 2018, and between them they collected $5,599.6 million that year. For comparison, Michigan is the only other state published here with a city tax layer, and it has a small fraction of that number.

Three rules decide what it costs you, and all three come from that table:

  • It follows the work, not just the home. The tax is “imposed on wages, salaries, and other compensation earned by residents and nonresidents who work in the municipality”. Commuting in does not put you outside it.
  • You are usually not taxed twice. “Most municipalities allow a partial or full credit to residents for municipal income taxes paid to another municipality where they are employed” — but it is the municipality’s choice, not a statewide guarantee.
  • 1% is the ceiling a city can set on its own. State law “requires that the rate must be uniform within a municipality and cannot exceed one percent without approval by voters”. Anything above 1% was voted for locally.

The scale, in money, on a $75,000 salary. At the 1% a city can set without a vote, it owes its city $750.00 — about half what the state takes. Across the range the Department’s table records, 0.50% to 3%, it is between $375.00 and $2,250.00. The crossover is 2.16%: above that rate your city takes more of your pay than Ohio does, and 61 of the 642 municipalities in the Department’s table are.

The shape of that table says something the range alone does not. 259 of the 642 — more than a third — sit at exactly 1.000%, the ceiling a council can set without asking its voters. The median rate is 1.5%, and 181 municipalities are at 2% or above. So on a $75,000 salary the typical Ohio city takes rather less than the state, while the 61 above the crossover take more — which is why no single number can stand in for your own city.

Two honest caveats. Those counts and rates are for calendar year 2018: it is the most recent municipality-by-municipality table the Department publishes, and we checked for later editions before saying so. And we deliberately do not name any city’s rate here, because individual cities have changed theirs since. Since “administration of the municipal income tax is strictly local”, the reliable sources for your own rate are your pay stub, your city’s income tax office, and the Department’s Finder tool at thefinder.tax.ohio.gov. None of it is in the figures on this page.

Ohio school districts levy their own income tax too

Separately from any city tax, Ohio school districts may levy an income tax on residents, filed on its own return, the SD 100. The Department’s IT 1040 booklet lists the rate for every taxing district as a four-digit decimal; the rates run from 0.25% to 2%. Districts use one of two bases, and the booklet marks which: a “traditional” base of modified adjusted gross income less exemptions, or an “earned income” base that counts only wages and self-employment income.

On a $75,000 salary that is between $187.50 and $1,500.00 a year, depending on the district — at the top of the range, close to what the state itself takes. The Department’s Finder tool at tax.ohio.gov/Finder tells you which district you live in. None of it is in the figures on this page.

Social Security stops, Medicare does not

Social Security is withheld at 6.2% until your wages reach $184,500 in 2026, then stops for the rest of the year. Medicare has no ceiling at all: 1.45% on every dollar, plus an extra 0.9% on wages above $200,000. This is why take-home pay rises unevenly through the year for high earners — the paycheck after you cross the Social Security wage base is visibly larger.

Common mistakes people make

Using an old Ohio bracket table

The most expensive error, and the easiest to make, because Ohio changed its schedule several years running. A table showing five brackets and a top rate near 4% describes a law that no longer exists. For 2026 there is one rate, 2.75%, above one threshold, $26,050.

Multiplying the whole salary by 2.75%

It overstates the tax at every income, because the exemption and the $26,050 threshold both come off first. On $50,000 that arithmetic gives $1,375.00 where the real figure is $931.50. On $75,000 it gives $2,062.50 against $1,619.00.

Forgetting the $332 step

The opposite error, and it only bites near the threshold. Someone earning just over $28,450 who applies 2.75% to the excess alone gets a few cents where the true answer is $332.03. The $332 is owed in full the moment the threshold is passed.

Treating the state tax as the whole tax

In Ohio this is the mistake that costs the most, and the arithmetic says so plainly. The state layer is genuinely small — $1,619.00 on $75,000. A city levying the 1% it can set without a vote takes $750.00 on the same salary, and 642 Ohio municipalities levy something. Add a school district levy on top and an Ohio budget built on the state figure alone is wrong by more than the state figure itself.

Expecting the calculator to match the paystub to the dollar

It will not, and in Ohio it particularly will not, because the state withholding tables take 3.4% where the tax is 2.75%, and because city tax is withheld alongside it. Treat this as an accurate model of what you owe at the federal and state level, and read why your pay stub will differ for the rest.

Example calculation

A single filer earning $75,000 in Ohio in 2026, claiming one exemption, with no retirement contribution:

  • Federal taxable income: $75,000 − $16,100 = $58,900
  • Federal income tax: $7,670.00
  • Social Security: $75,000 × 6.2% = $4,650.00
  • Medicare: $75,000 × 1.45% = $1,087.50
  • Ohio exemption at this income: $2,150 (one person, income between $40,001 and $80,000)
  • Ohio taxable income: $75,000 − $2,150 = $72,850
  • Ohio income tax: $332 + 2.75% × ($72,850 − $26,050) = $1,619.00
  • Total withheld: $15,026.50
  • Take-home pay: $59,973.50 a year, or $4,997.79 a month
  • Effective tax rate: 20.0%

Add a city income tax and, if your district levies one, a school district tax, and the picture changes materially — which is exactly why both are called out above rather than buried.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Ohio income tax rate in 2026?

2.75%, and it applies only to the part of your taxable income above $26,050. Below that, Ohio charges nothing. The rate sits in the statute itself, Ohio Revised Code section 5747.02: “For taxable years beginning in 2026 and thereafter, $332.00 plus 2.75% of the amount in excess of $26,050.” Ohio used to have five brackets topping out near 4%; that structure is gone, and calculators still showing it are years out of date.

At what salary does Ohio income tax actually start?

About $28,450 for a single filer, and $30,850 for a married couple filing jointly with two exemptions. Ohio taxes nothing on the first $26,050 of taxable income, and taxable income is your pay minus $2,400 for every exemption you claim. Add the two together and you have the point where the state starts taking anything at all. On $25,000 a single Ohioan pays the state $0.00.

Is it true that one extra dollar can cost $332 in Ohio?

Yes, and it is in the law rather than a rounding artefact. The statute says nothing is owed at or below $26,050 of taxable income, then “$332.00 plus 2.75% of the amount in excess of $26,050”. So at $28,450 of salary a single filer owes $0.00, and at $28,451 they owe $332.03. That one dollar costs $332.03. Ohio's own IT 1040 booklet describes it the same way: its worked example says the taxpayer “owes $342 on the first $26,050 of income” for 2025, even though the row above it reads 0.000%. The base falls to $332 for 2026.

What is take-home pay on a $75,000 salary in Ohio?

About $59,974 a year, or $4,997.79 a month, for a single filer with no retirement contribution. That is after $7,670 of federal income tax, $5,738 of Social Security and Medicare and only $1,619.00 of Ohio income tax — a state rate of 2.16%. Of the states published here that levy an income tax at all, Ohio leaves you the most. School district and municipal income taxes are not included.

Why does my Ohio employer withhold more than I owe?

Because the withholding tables and the tax are two different things in Ohio. The Department's own withholding tables effective August 1, 2026 say: “If the wages exceed $1,923, use the last row of the table plus 3.400% of the excess over $1,923.” That is a 3.4% withholding rate against a 2.75% tax. On $75,000 the gap is roughly $931 over a year. It comes back as a refund when you file, but it is money you do not have during the year.

Does a 401(k) contribution lower my Ohio tax?

Yes. Ohio taxable income starts from your federal adjusted gross income, and an elective deferral is already out of that figure. On $75,000 with 6% going into a 401(k), your Ohio tax falls by $123.75. That is a small number because the rate is small — the same contribution saves far more federal tax. The rule is not universal either: in Pennsylvania the same worker's state tax falls by $0.00, nothing at all.

How much is the Ohio personal exemption in 2026?

$2,400 per person if your income is $40,000 or less, $2,150 between $40,001 and $80,000, and $1,900 above that — claimed for yourself, your spouse and each dependant. Two things to know. The amount falls as you earn more, which no other state on this site does. And for 2026 the exemption disappears entirely above $500,000 of income, down from $750,000 in 2025.

What is $20 an hour after taxes in Ohio?

At 40 hours a week, $20 an hour is $41,600 a year gross and about $34,905 after tax, which works out at $16.78 an hour in real terms. Ohio takes only $700.50 of that for the year — 1.68% of gross. Federal tax and FICA take far more.

Do Ohio school districts charge their own income tax?

Many do, and it is separate from both the state tax and any city tax. The Department's IT 1040 booklet lists the rate for each district as a four-digit decimal; the rates run from 0.25% to 2%. Districts also use one of two bases: a “traditional” base of modified adjusted gross income less exemptions, or an “earned income” base that looks only at wages. It is filed on a separate return, the SD 100. On $75,000 a school district levy is worth between $187.50 and $1,500.00 a year, and none of it is in the figures on this page. Use the Department's Finder tool at tax.ohio.gov/Finder to see which district you are in.

Why is my Ohio paycheck smaller than this calculator says?

In Ohio there are usually three reasons, in this order. Your city may well levy a municipal income tax — 642 Ohio municipalities did so in 2018, per the Department's own Table LG-11 — which is administered by the city rather than the state and is not modelled here. A city may set up to 1% on its own authority and more if its voters approve, so on $75,000 that is $750.00 a year at the 1% floor and more where voters have gone higher: 61 of the 642 municipalities charge above 2.16%, the rate at which a city takes more of a $75,000 salary than Ohio does. Your school district may levy one too. And the state withholding tables take more than the tax you owe, which you get back only at filing. After that come the ordinary causes: health premiums and other benefit deductions come out before tax, and a second job pushes federal withholding up.
