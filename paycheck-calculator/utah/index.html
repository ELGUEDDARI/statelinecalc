Utah taxes income at a flat 4.45% in 2026, down from 4.5%. On $75,000, a single filer pays $3,337.50 to the state and keeps about $58,255 a year.

How this calculator works

Utah looks like the simplest state tax in the country and is not quite. There is one rate, 4.45%, and no brackets. But there is also no deduction and no personal exemption: the rate is applied to your entire wage, and relief arrives afterwards as a credit against the tax itself. That credit shrinks as you earn more, which means Utah’s real rate rises with income even though its headline rate never moves.

The calculator applies four deductions, in this order:

  • Federal income tax. Your gross pay minus the federal standard deduction for your filing status, run through the 2026 federal brackets. For 2026 that deduction is $16,100 single, $32,200 married filing jointly, $24,150 head of household.
  • Social Security at 6.2% of gross wages, up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500.
  • Medicare at 1.45% of all wages, with no cap, plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on wages above $200,000.
  • Utah income tax at a flat 4.45% of your whole wage, less the taxpayer credit: $485 for a single filer, reduced by 1.3% of every dollar above $9,348, and never taken below zero.

Rates come from the agencies that set them: the IRS for the federal brackets, the standard deduction and FICA, cross-checked against the Social Security Administration for the wage base. The Utah rate was verified on two independent official sources, because the first one we read was out of date. Utah Code section 59-10-104, the statute itself, sets the tax at your state taxable income multiplied by “4.45%”, and is marked “Amended by Chapter 250, 2026 General Session”. The Utah State Tax Commission’s Publication 14, Withholding Tax Guide, revision 4/26 and effective for pay periods beginning on or after June 1, 2026, uses the same rate on every one of its eight schedules and supplies the credit figures. Both were read on September 2, 2026. Our full sourcing is on the methodology page.

A warning about the state’s own rate page. On the day we wrote this, the Tax Commission’s income tax rate page still read “January 1, 2025 – current: 4.5% or .045”. That page had not been updated for the 2026 cut. The law and the withholding guide agree on 4.45%, and that is the figure used here. If another calculator shows you 4.5% for 2026, this is almost certainly why.

What the calculator deliberately does not do: it does not itemize deductions, model the dependant exemption or the Utah retirement credit, handle multiple jobs, or account for health insurance premiums and other employer benefit deductions.

Utah hourly paycheck: what your rate is worth after tax

Why we ask your hours instead of assuming 2,080

Most hourly calculators multiply your rate by 2,080 and call it a year. That is 40 hours a week for 52 weeks, which describes a full-time salaried schedule rather than most hourly work. If you are on 32 hours, or 45 with overtime, the assumed figure is wrong before any tax is applied. The calculator above asks for your hours and uses them.

What $20 an hour comes to in Utah

At 40 hours a week, $20 an hour is $41,600 a year gross. After federal tax, Social Security, Medicare and Utah’s 4.45%, that leaves about $33,820 a year, or $2,818.34 a month. Your real hourly rate — what an hour of work actually puts in your account — is $16.26. Utah’s share is $1,785.48 for the year, which is less than 4.45% of your gross because the taxpayer credit is still worth $65.72 at this salary.

What $25 and $30 an hour come to

$25 an hour is $52,000 gross and about $41,648 after tax, a real rate of $20.02. $30 an hour is $62,400 gross and about $49,542 after tax, a real rate of $24. Utah’s credit runs out between the two lower rates: $46,656 a year falls between $20 an hour ($41,600) and $25 an hour ($52,000). So a worker on $25 or $30 an hour pays a clean 4.45% on every dollar, while a worker on $20 an hour still has $66 of credit left.

Overtime, tips and shift differentials

Utah taxes overtime, tips and shift differentials at the same 4.45% as your base pay. There is a second, quieter effect: overtime raises your annual wage, which shrinks whatever is left of the taxpayer credit at 1.3 cents on the dollar. Below $46,656 a year, an extra hour of overtime is effectively taxed by Utah at 5.75% rather than 4.45% — the rate plus the credit you lose. Above that line the credit is already gone and the marginal rate is the headline one.

Key facts that affect your take-home pay in Utah

The rate is 4.45% in 2026, not 4.5%

Utah has cut its income tax rate almost every year, and 2026 is another cut: from 4.5% to 4.45%. The number is in the statute — Utah Code 59-10-104, amended by Chapter 250 of the 2026 General Session — and in Publication 14, the Tax Commission’s own withholding guide, on all eight of its payroll schedules. On a $75,000 salary the cut is worth $37.50 a year, which is real money but smaller than most people expect a rate cut to be.

Utah has no deduction and no exemption — it has a credit

This is the structural fact that makes Utah different from every other state published here, and the one most calculators get wrong. Michigan subtracts $5,900 from your income before taxing it. Illinois subtracts $2,925. Utah subtracts nothing: the 4.45% applies to your whole wage. Relief comes afterwards, as a taxpayer credit deducted from the tax itself.

For 2026 the credit is $485 a year for a single filer and $970 for a married couple filing jointly — exactly double, at every payroll period. It is reduced by 1.3 cents for every dollar of wages above $9,348 single or $18,696 joint, and it is never allowed to push your tax below zero. Publication 14’s annual schedule states it in six lines: multiply wages by .0445, take a base allowance of 485, subtract 1.3% of wages over $9,348 from that allowance, then subtract what is left from the tax.

The credit runs out at $46,656, and that is where Utah becomes a flat tax

Because the credit fades at a fixed 1.3 cents on the dollar, the point where it hits zero is arithmetic rather than policy: $485 divided by 1.3% is $37,308, added to the $9,348 threshold, gives $46,656 for a single filer and $93,311 for a couple. Below that line your effective Utah rate is under 4.45%. Above it, Utah is exactly the flat tax it advertises, for everyone, forever.

Your effective Utah rate rises with income, then stops dead

On $25,000 the state takes $830.98, an effective state rate of 3.32%. On $40,000 it takes $1,693.48, or 4.23%. On $75,000 it is 4.45%, and on $250,000 it is still 4.45% — the same, because the credit ran out long before. That flat top is unusual: in Michigan, where relief is a fixed-dollar exemption, the effective rate keeps creeping toward the headline rate at every income and never quite arrives.

Your 401(k) contribution does reduce your Utah tax

Publication 14 defines the base without ambiguity: “Utah calculates withholding tax based on wages subject to federal withholding tax (as defined by the IRS).” An elective deferral to a 401(k) or 403(b) is excluded from those wages, so it cuts your state tax as well as your federal tax. On $75,000 with 6% going in, your Utah tax falls by $200.25 a year.

It is worth saying explicitly because the rule is not universal: a Pennsylvania worker making the same contribution on the same salary sees their state tax fall by $0.00 — Pennsylvania treats the deferral as compensation the moment it is made. Utah does not.

Utah withholding ignores your W-4 allowances entirely

Publication 14 is explicit: “No subtraction is made for personal or other withholding allowances claimed on federal form W-4.” Two Utah workers on the same wage and the same filing status have the same state withholding, whatever their household looks like. Dependants affect your annual TC-40 return, not the amount the employer takes out each payday.

No city or county income tax comes out of a Utah paycheck

Publication 14 sets out one withholding and one only, the state income tax, with no local layer anywhere in its schedules. That is a real difference from several of the states published here: a Detroit resident pays 2.4% to the city on top of Michigan’s state rate, and local earned income tax is routine in Pennsylvania. Utah’s sales and property tax rates do vary a great deal between cities and counties, but neither is withheld from your pay.

Social Security stops, Medicare does not

Social Security is withheld at 6.2% until your wages reach $184,500 in 2026, then stops for the rest of the year. Medicare has no ceiling at all: 1.45% on every dollar, plus an extra 0.9% on wages above $200,000. This is why take-home pay rises unevenly through the year for high earners — the paycheck after you cross the Social Security wage base is visibly larger.

Common mistakes people make

Using 4.5% because that is what the state’s rate page says

It is out of date. The Tax Commission’s rate page still showed “January 1, 2025 – current: 4.5%” in September 2026, while the statute and the withholding guide both say 4.45%. On $75,000 the difference is $37.50 a year — small, but wrong in the direction that makes you budget for less than you get.

Looking for a Utah standard deduction

There is not one, and the search usually ends badly. People find the federal figure and subtract $16,100 from their wage before applying 4.45%, which understates their Utah tax by $716.45. Utah taxes the whole wage and gives the relief back as a credit instead.

Multiplying the whole salary by 4.45% and stopping there

The opposite error, and it only bites below $46,656. On $25,000 that arithmetic gives $1,112.50 when the real figure is $830.98 — you have forgotten $281.52 of credit. On $40,000 it gives $1,780.00 against a real $1,693.48, forgetting $86.52. Above $46,656 the shortcut is exactly right, which is part of why the mistake survives.

Giving a married couple a single filer’s credit

A couple filing jointly gets $970, not $485, and their phase-out does not start until $18,696. On a $60,000 joint income the credit is still worth $433.05, where a single filer on the same wage has had nothing left for years. Halving the household figures is the most expensive shortcut on this page.

Expecting the calculator to match the paystub to the dollar

It will not, and no calculator can. Health premiums and benefit deductions come out first, your federal W-4 settings drive the federal share, and a second job changes everything. Treat this as an accurate model of the federal and state layers, and read why your pay stub will differ for the rest.

Example calculation

A single filer earning $75,000 in Utah in 2026, with no retirement contribution:

  • Federal taxable income: $75,000 − $16,100 = $58,900
  • Federal income tax: $7,670.00
  • Social Security: $75,000 × 6.2% = $4,650.00
  • Medicare: $75,000 × 1.45% = $1,087.50
  • Utah tax before credit: $75,000 × 4.45% = $3,337.50
  • Taxpayer credit: $485 − 1.3% × ($75,000 − $9,348) = $0, fully phased out
  • Utah income tax: $3,337.50
  • Total withheld: $16,745.00
  • Take-home pay: $58,255.00 a year, or $4,854.58 a month
  • Effective tax rate: 22.3%

The same worker on $25,000 would keep $281.52 of the credit, paying $830.98 to Utah instead of the $1,112.50 a flat 4.45% would suggest.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Utah income tax rate in 2026?

A flat 4.45%. Utah cut the rate from 4.5% for 2026, and the figure is written into the law itself: Utah Code section 59-10-104 sets the tax at the taxpayer's state taxable income multiplied by “4.45%”, amended by Chapter 250 of the 2026 General Session. The Tax Commission's own withholding guide, Publication 14 revision 4/26, uses the same number on every schedule: “Multiply line 1 by .0445 (4.45%)”. Be careful with the rate page on the state's website, which still showed 4.5% in September 2026.

Does Utah have a standard deduction?

No, and this is the part almost every Utah calculation gets wrong. Utah does not shrink your taxable income at all: it charges 4.45% on your full wages, then subtracts a taxpayer credit. For 2026 the credit starts at $485 a year for a single filer, and it fades by 1.3 cents for every dollar you earn above $9,348. It is gone completely at $46,656. A married couple filing jointly gets $970, fading above $18,696 and gone at $93,311.

What is take-home pay on a $75,000 salary in Utah?

About $58,255 a year, or $4,854.58 a month, for a single filer with no retirement contribution. That is after $7,670 of federal income tax, $5,738 of Social Security and Medicare and $3,337.50 of Utah income tax — an effective rate of 22.3%. At this salary the taxpayer credit is long gone, so the Utah figure is a clean 4.45% of gross pay.

Why is my Utah effective tax rate lower than 4.45%?

Because the taxpayer credit has not run out yet. Below $46,656 single, the credit still takes something off your bill, so your real state rate sits under the headline one. On $25,000 Utah takes $830.98, which is 3.32%. On $40,000 it takes $1,693.48, or 4.23%. From $46,656 upwards it is exactly 4.45% and stays there — Utah is only a true flat tax above that line.

Does a 401(k) contribution lower my Utah tax?

Yes. Publication 14 defines the base plainly: “Utah calculates withholding tax based on wages subject to federal withholding tax (as defined by the IRS).” An elective deferral to a 401(k) or 403(b) is already out of that figure, so it reduces your Utah tax as well as your federal tax. On $75,000 with 6% going in, your Utah tax falls by $200.25. The rule is not universal: in Pennsylvania the same worker's state tax falls by $0.00 — nothing at all.

What is $20 an hour after taxes in Utah?

At 40 hours a week, $20 an hour is $41,600 a year gross and about $33,820 after tax, which works out at $16.26 an hour in real terms. Utah takes $1,785.48 of that. At this salary the taxpayer credit is still worth $65.72, which is why the state's share is below 4.45% of gross.

Do any Utah cities charge their own income tax?

Publication 14 provides for one withholding only — the state income tax — and sets out no city or county income tax withholding anywhere in its schedules. That is a meaningful difference from states like Michigan, where Detroit adds 2.4% on top of the state layer, or Pennsylvania, where local earned income tax is normal. Utah's sales and property taxes do vary a great deal by locality, but neither comes out of your paycheck.

How does head of household work in Utah?

Utah's withholding schedules have only two columns, Single and Married, so an employer withholds from a head of household on the Single schedule. That is what this calculator does, and it is the number that will appear on your pay stub. When you file your annual TC-40 return, the taxpayer credit phase-out threshold for head of household sits between the single and married figures, so your final bill may come out slightly lower than shown here.

Why is my Utah paycheck smaller than this calculator says?

Health insurance premiums and other benefit deductions come out before tax and are not included here; your federal W-4 may be set differently from the filing status assumed; and a second job pushes your withholding up. Note also that Utah withholding does not respond to allowances at all — Publication 14 states that “No subtraction is made for personal or other withholding allowances claimed on federal form W-4”, so the state layer is the same for everyone on the same wage and filing status.

Compare with other states

The same $75,000 salary, single filer, 2026 rates, state layer only:

  • Michigan — $58,656 take-home, an effective rate of 21.8%. A Michigan worker keeps $400.75 more than a Utahn on the same salary.
  • Illinois — $58,025 take-home, an effective rate of 22.6%. An Illinois worker keeps $230.21 less than a Utahn on the same salary.
  • Pennsylvania — $59,238 take-home, an effective rate of 21.0%. A Pennsylvania worker keeps $982.50 more than a Utahn on the same salary.
  • Nevada — $61,593 take-home, an effective rate of 17.9%. A Nevada worker keeps $3,337.50 more than a Utahn on the same salary.

At $75,000 only Illinois takes more of the states published here, because Utah’s credit has run out by this salary and the full 4.45% applies to every dollar. Lower down the scale the ranking changes: the credit is worth more, proportionally, to a worker on $25,000 than Michigan’s exemption is worth to the same worker. The states that levy nothing at all remain in a category of their own.
