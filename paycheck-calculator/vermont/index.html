Vermont Paycheck Calculator 2026

Vermont withholds 3.35%, 6.6%, 7.6% or 8.75% of the pay left after a $5,400 allowance, with nothing withheld on the first $3,925 of that. On $75,000, a single filer keeps about $58,907 a year; we found no required state payroll deduction. At $25 an hour, full time, you keep about $20.45 an hour.

How this calculator works

The Vermont Department of Taxes publishes a withholding method, and this calculator follows it. Your pay is converted to a yearly amount. The withholding allowances come off ($5,400 for a single filer, $10,800 for a married filer whose spouse does not work). The rest is taxed through a table with a 0% step, then 3.35%, 6.6%, 7.6% and 8.75% steps, and the yearly result is divided by the number of pay periods. Vermont prints two tables, one for single filers and one for married filers, and the married table has wider steps.

The calculator applies these deductions in this order:

  • Federal income tax. Your gross pay minus the federal standard deduction for your filing status, run through the 2026 federal brackets.
  • Social Security at 6.2% of gross wages, up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500.
  • Medicare at 1.45% of all wages, with no cap, plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on wages above $200,000.
  • Vermont income tax: your wages after any 401(k) contribution, minus the Vermont withholding allowances for your status, taxed through the table.

This calculator has no other Vermont line. We looked for a state payroll deduction that an employer must take from your check and found none. Two optional ones are covered below: part of the Child Care Contribution, and a voluntary leave plan.

Rates come from the agencies that set them: the IRS for the federal brackets, the standard deduction and FICA, cross-checked against the Social Security Administration for the wage base; and, for Vermont, the Department of Taxes’ withholding guide, Form W-4VT and Child Care Contribution pages, and the Governor’s page for the paid leave program. Our full sourcing is on the methodology page.

How current the source is. The guide is document GB-1210-2026, and its cover reads “effective January 1, 2026 - expires December 31, 2026.” We read it on the Department’s own site on October 20, 2026, and the Sources section links to it. The Form W-4VT the Department posts is dated “Rev. 12/18” in its footer; its allowance amounts are not in the form, they come from the guide, which says one allowance equals $5,400.00 a year. The federal figures, Social Security wage base and Medicare thresholds are the 2026 ones.

How we checked the formula against the printed tables. The guide prints the withholding for hundreds of pay ranges and for 0 to 10 allowances, in wage-bracket charts for weekly, biweekly, twice-a-month, monthly and daily pay, one chart for single filers and one for married filers. We rebuilt 9,130 of those printed cells from the formula, using the middle of each pay range. 9,085 matched exactly, and the other 45 are off by rounding. We left out the chart rows whose columns do not read cleanly in the file. We also checked 105 printed breakpoints, allowance amounts and base amounts: each base amount equals the one before it plus the tax on the step, and each breakpoint equals the yearly one divided by the number of pay periods. In the guide’s own worked example, a married employee with two allowances paid $1,800 a week has $45.77 withheld, and the weekly table reproduces it.

Withholding is an estimate, not your final bill. The figure here is what an employer takes from each check using the Department’s method. Your actual Vermont income tax is figured on your return, so what you owe or get back when you file can differ.

What the calculator deliberately leaves out: dependents on Form W-4VT, a spouse who works, the “higher single rate” box, extra withholding and the “Exempt” line, bonuses, rounding by your employer, work in another state, optional employer deductions, and health insurance premiums and other employer benefit deductions.

Vermont’s five steps and the pay they apply to

The Department’s yearly percentage table has five steps, and they apply to what is left of your pay after the allowances, not to your pay itself. For a single filer on $75,000, that is $75,000 − $5,400 = $69,600. The first $3,925 of it is not taxed, the next $50,750 is taxed at 3.35%, and the rest, $14,925, falls in the 6.6% step, which runs up to $126,775. The 8.75% step starts above $260,225 of taxable pay, which for a single filer is $265,625 of yearly pay.

The withholding allowance: what changes with your Vermont W-4VT

The Department’s withholding method uses allowances rather than a standard deduction, and Form W-4VT is how you tell your employer how many to apply. Each one is worth $5,400 a year. This is how the calculator maps its three choices.

If you cross a state line: how Vermont withholds

Vermont borders New Hampshire, Massachusetts and New York, and some people live on one side of a state line and work on the other. Rules apply in both states, and this calculator models none of them. It assumes you live and work in Vermont.

If you live in Vermont and work outside it

The guide says that when a payment to a Vermont resident includes pay for work done outside Vermont, the withholding is computed on the full payment and then reduced by the income tax withheld for the state where the work was done. It also says an employee who moves into Vermont during a tax year is a resident for withholding purposes.

If you live elsewhere and work in Vermont

Wages paid to a nonresident for services performed in Vermont are subject to Vermont withholding if they are subject to federal withholding. For a nonresident who works in Vermont and another state during a pay period, the guide computes the tax on the full payment and multiplies it by the ratio of Vermont hours to total hours. Its example: 16 hours in Vermont out of a 40-hour pay period, with $48.00 of Vermont withholding on the whole pay, makes $19.20.

If you work from home in Vermont for an employer in another state

The Department says an out-of-state employer does not have to begin withholding Vermont income tax until an employee has been working from a Vermont location for thirty days. Income earned during the entire period of living in Vermont is subject to Vermont income tax, even if the employee claims another state as home; the thirty days only decide when the employer must start withholding. The Department says employers should work with these employees, and the W-4VT helps them set their allowances.

Two optional deductions: the Child Care Contribution and paid leave

The Child Care Contribution is an employer tax, with an option to share part of it

Vermont employers pay a 0.44% payroll tax on wages called the Child Care Contribution, on wages paid on or after July 1, 2024. The Department says an employer may choose to deduct and withhold a maximum of 25 percent of it, which is 0.11% of the employee’s wages, and the employer may withhold a smaller portion or none at all. Employers do not have to treat every employee the same way, and employees have no filing requirement.

What the maximum withholding would cost you

On $75,000, 0.11% is $82.50 a year, or $6.88 a month. Because nothing requires your employer to take it, this calculator does not subtract it. If your employer does, your take-home pay on $75,000 would be about $58,825 instead of $58,907. The Department’s Child Care Contribution page says an employer that withholds part of it reports the amount on the employee’s W-2.

Paid family and medical leave is voluntary

The Governor’s page for the Vermont Family and Medical Leave plan describes “a voluntary paid family and medical leave program.” Employers can choose to design a plan, and the contributions can be paid by the employer, split between the employer and employees, or paid by employees as a voluntary benefit. We found no Vermont paid leave deduction that every employee must pay. If your employer offers a plan and deducts a premium, it will appear on your stub, and this calculator does not include it.

Vermont hourly paycheck: what your rate is worth after tax

Why we ask for your hours instead of assuming 2,080

Many hourly calculators multiply your rate by 2,080 and call it a year. That is 40 hours a week for 52 weeks, which describes a full-time schedule, not every hourly job. If you work 32 hours a week, or 45 with overtime, the assumed figure is wrong before any tax is applied. The calculator above asks for your hours and uses them. In Vermont your hours also matter because of the allowance: a single filer earning $9,325 or less a year has no Vermont tax withheld.

What is $20 an hour after taxes in Vermont?

At 40 hours a week, $20 an hour is $41,600 a year gross. After federal tax, Social Security, Medicare and Vermont income tax withholding, that leaves about $34,524 a year, or $2,877.03 a month. Your real hourly rate — what an hour of work actually puts in your bank account — is $16.60. Vermont’s share is $1,081.21 of income tax withholding for the year.

What are $25 and $30 an hour after taxes in Vermont?

$25 an hour is $52,000 gross and about $42,532 after tax, a real rate of $20.45. $30 an hour is $62,400 gross and about $50,465 after tax, a real rate of $24.26. Vermont income tax withholding is $1,429.61 a year at $25 an hour, and $1,853.58 at $30.

Overtime and bonuses

The calculator multiplies your rate by the hours you enter and adds no overtime premium, and it does not model bonuses. The guide says that for non-periodic payments, the Vermont withholding can be estimated at 30% of the federal withholding, and it does not say whether a bonus counts as one. Ask your employer how it withholds on a bonus. If you work variable hours, enter the average number of hours you expect to work each week.

What this calculator does not cover

This tool models federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare and the payroll programs your state actually runs — nothing more. It deliberately leaves out:

  • Itemized deductions and federal tax credits — the Child Tax Credit, the Earned Income Tax Credit, education credits. The engine always applies the standard deduction; there is no itemizing path.
  • A second job, self-employment income or 1099 pay. Every dollar you enter is treated as one employer’s W-2 wages, taxed at the employee share of Social Security and Medicare — not the self-employment tax a 1099 worker actually owes.
  • Pre-tax benefits other than the 401(k) field — health insurance premiums, an HSA or FSA, commuter benefits. The only amount taken out before tax is the percentage you enter for a 401(k).
  • Local taxes — the Vermont withholding guide we read describes state withholding only, and we found no Vermont city or county income tax on wages in it. That is not proof that none exists. This calculator adds no local line for Vermont. The only local income tax lines on this site are on the Indiana and Maryland calculators.
  • Overtime premiums. An hourly rate is multiplied by the hours you enter; it does not add a time-and-a-half rate for hours worked past 40 in a week.
  • Wage garnishments or court-ordered deductions — child support, a wage levy. Your result assumes none exist.
  • The Additional Medicare Tax, at the single-filer threshold. The 0.9% surtax above $200,000 is applied at that one figure for every filing status, the way an employer actually withholds it — not the higher threshold a married couple filing jointly may end up owing at tax return time.
  • Married filing separately, or a qualifying surviving spouse. The calculator only offers single, married filing jointly, and head of household.

None of these are in the estimate. Some of them change what you owe or get back when you file, and a calculator cannot ask for everything they depend on. See why your pay stub will differ for the details.

Key facts that affect your take-home pay in Vermont

The first $3,925 of what is left is not taxed

The table starts with a 0% step. With the $5,400 allowance, a single filer’s yearly pay has to pass $9,325 before Vermont withholds anything. At $17,000, Vermont withholds $257.11; at $30,000, it withholds $692.61 a year.

The 6.6% step starts at $60,075 of pay

The 3.35% step covers $50,750 of taxable pay and produces $1,700.13 of tax. The 6.6% step covers the next $72,100 and adds $4,758.60. For a single filer, 6.6% starts at $60,075 of yearly pay and ends at $132,175.

The 7.6% and 8.75% steps start higher

For a single filer, 7.6% applies to taxable pay from $126,775 to $260,225, which is pay from $132,175 to $265,625. Above that, every dollar of taxable pay is taxed at 8.75%. On $400,000, taxable pay is $394,600, $134,375 of it is in the top step, and Vermont withholds $28,358.74.

The married table is wider, and the allowance doubles

A single filer’s taxable pay is pay minus $5,400. A married filer whose spouse does not work, with two allowances, gets $10,800 off and a table whose 0% step runs to $11,775 and whose 6.6% step starts above $96,475. That is why Vermont withholds $1,756.24 from that filer on $75,000 and $2,685.18 from a single one. Taxable pay for the married filer is $64,200, all of it in the 3.35% step after the first $11,775.

Head of household: two allowances and, in our reading, the single table

With lines 1 and 4 of the W-4VT worksheet, a head of household has two allowances, $10,800. We use the single table, our reading of a form that has no head of household box. On $75,000, taxable pay is $64,200 and Vermont withholds $2,328.78. The federal tax is lower for a head of household ($5,748.00 on $75,000), so take-home pay is $61,186.

Each allowance is worth up to $472.50 a year

Line 3 of the worksheet asks for the number of dependents you plan to claim, and each one adds an allowance of $5,400. In the 6.6% step that lowers yearly withholding by $356.40; in the 3.35% step it is $180.90, and in the top step $472.50. This calculator does not ask for dependents, so a worker with children who claims them on the W-4VT will see less Vermont tax withheld than the figure shown. The form adds that if you and your spouse both claim your dependents, not enough tax will be withheld.

Without a W-4VT, your employer may use your federal W-4

The guide says an employer may use the information from the federal Form W-4 if a Vermont form is not submitted, and that not enough tax might be withheld that way. It says employees who adjusted their federal withholding in anticipation of a credit, and employees in civil unions or civil marriages, will not have the correct Vermont tax withheld unless they complete a W-4VT. The Department strongly recommends that employers require one.

Extra withholding: about 30% of the federal extra

If the federal W-4 shows an extra amount to withhold each pay period, the guide says Vermont’s withholding should be increased by 30% of that extra federal amount. The W-4VT makes the same suggestion for its own line for an additional amount. This calculator does not model extra withholding.

With two jobs, put the allowances where you earn the most

The form says that if you have more than one employer, you should consider claiming zero allowances with the employer where you earn less income. This calculator treats your pay as your only income, so it cannot show the effect.

Your 401(k) lowers your Vermont taxable wages in this calculator

The guide says Vermont withholding is calculated similarly to the federal one, and it gives no separate rule for 401(k) contributions. This calculator therefore starts from pay after a traditional 401(k) contribution, as it does in most states on this site. On $75,000 with 6% going in, Vermont income tax withholding falls by $297.00 a year. Enter 0 if your contribution is a Roth.

Social Security stops, Medicare does not

Social Security is withheld at 6.2% until your wages reach $184,500 in 2026, then stops for the rest of the year. Medicare has no ceiling at all: 1.45% on every dollar, plus an extra 0.9% on wages above $200,000. On $250,000, Social Security and Medicare take $15,514.00 in total.

Common mistakes

Forgetting the allowance

Applying the table to the full $75,000 instead of $69,600 overstates Vermont withholding by $356.40: $3,041.58 instead of $2,685.18.

Taxing everything at one rate

The 6.6% rate applies only to the part of taxable pay between $54,675 and $126,775 for a single filer. Applying it to all of $69,600 gives $4,593.60, which overstates withholding by about $1,908.

Claiming two allowances when your spouse works

Line 2 of the W-4VT worksheet is only for a spouse who does not work. If both of you work and one of you claims it anyway, one allowance too many comes off. On $75,000 that lowers withholding from $1,937.14 to $1,756.24, which is $180.90 too little withheld over a year. The shortfall can show up as a balance due when you file.

Both spouses claiming the dependents

The form says that if you and your spouse both claim your dependents on your own W-4VTs, not enough income tax will be withheld, and you might owe when you file. Only one of you should claim them.

Assuming the Child Care Contribution is always yours to pay

It is an employer tax. An employer may take up to 0.11% of your wages toward it, or nothing. If your stub shows a deduction for it, that is your employer’s choice, and the most it can be on $75,000 is $82.50 a year.

Expecting the calculator to match the pay stub to the dollar

It will not, and no calculator can. Health insurance premiums and other pre-tax benefit deductions come out before tax and are not modeled here, and the allowances you claimed on your Form W-4VT may differ from the ones we assume. Read why your pay stub will differ for the rest.

Example calculation

Amounts are shown to the cent, so adding the rounded lines can differ from the total by a cent.

A single filer earning $75,000 in Vermont in 2026, with one allowance and no retirement contribution, under the Department’s method:

  • Federal taxable income: $75,000 − federal standard deduction of $16,100 = $58,900
  • Federal income tax: $7,670.00
  • Social Security: $75,000 × 6.2% = $4,650.00
  • Medicare: $75,000 × 1.45% = $1,087.50
  • Vermont taxable pay: $75,000 − $5,400 allowance = $69,600
  • Tax on the first $3,925: $3,925 × 0% = $0.00
  • Tax on the next $50,750: $50,750 × 3.35% = $1,700.13
  • Tax on the remaining $14,925: $14,925 × 6.6% = $985.05
  • Vermont income tax withholding: $1,700.13 + $985.05 = $2,685.18 a year, $223.76 on a monthly paycheck or $103.28 on a biweekly one
  • Total withheld: $16,092.68
  • Take-home pay: $58,907.33 a year, or $4,908.94 a month
  • Effective tax rate: 21.5%

Frequently asked questions

What is the Vermont income tax withholding rate?

Vermont’s withholding table has four rates, 3.35%, 6.6%, 7.6% and 8.75%, on top of a first step at 0%. For a single filer, nothing is withheld on the first $3,925 of what is left after the allowance, 3.35% is withheld on the next $50,750, 6.6% on the next $72,100, 7.6% on the next $133,450, and 8.75% above $260,225. A married filer gets wider steps. “What is left” means your yearly pay minus the withholding allowances from your Form W-4VT, which are $5,400 each. On $75,000, a single filer has $2,685.18 withheld a year.

How much Vermont tax is withheld on a $75,000 salary?

For a single filer with one allowance, Vermont withholds $2,685.18 a year, which is $223.76 a month or $103.28 on a biweekly check. Taxable pay is $75,000 minus the $5,400 allowance, or $69,600. The first $3,925 of it is not taxed, the next $50,750 produces $1,700.13, and the remaining $14,925 is taxed at 6.6%. A married filer whose spouse does not work, with two allowances ($10,800) and the married table, has $1,756.24 withheld, and a head of household with two allowances has $2,328.78 withheld.

What is take-home pay on a $75,000 salary in Vermont?

About $58,907 a year, or $4,908.94 a month, for a single filer with no retirement contribution. That is after $7,670.00 of federal income tax, $5,738 of Social Security and Medicare, and $2,685.18 of Vermont income tax, an effective rate of 21.5%. We found no Vermont city or county income tax in the withholding guide, so there is no local line.

How much is $20, $25 or $30 an hour after taxes in Vermont?

At 40 hours a week, $20 an hour leaves about $34,524 a year, or $16.60 an hour. $25 an hour leaves about $42,532, or $20.45 an hour. $30 an hour leaves about $50,465, or $24.26 an hour. Those are single-filer figures after federal tax, FICA and Vermont income tax withholding, with no retirement contribution.

What is a Vermont withholding allowance?

It is the amount your employer subtracts from your pay before applying the Vermont table. The Department of Taxes’ guide says one withholding allowance equals $5,400.00 a year, and the number you claim comes from the worksheet on Form W-4VT: one for yourself if no one can claim you as a dependent, one more if you file jointly and your spouse does not work, one for each dependent you plan to claim, and one if you plan to file as head of household. Your actual Vermont tax is figured on your return, where the deductions and credits you actually qualify for apply.

How many allowances do I claim on the Vermont W-4VT if I am married?

It depends on your spouse’s work. Line 2 of the worksheet says to enter 1 if you are filing jointly and your spouse does not work. With line 1, that makes two allowances, $10,800 in all, and the married table applies. This calculator uses that case for “Married filing jointly.” If your spouse works, line 2 stays empty and you have one allowance. On $75,000, that would withhold $180.90 more Vermont tax a year than this calculator shows. The form also has a box for “Married, but withhold at higher single rate,” which the guide does not explain further and which we do not model.

How does head of household work on the Vermont W-4VT?

The worksheet adds one allowance on line 4 if you plan to file as head of household. With the one for yourself on line 1, that is two allowances, $10,800 in all. The filing-status line of the form lists Single, Married/Civil Union Filing Jointly, Married/Civil Union Filing Separately, and Married but withhold at higher single rate, with no head of household box. We assume a head of household checks Single and uses the single table, which is our reading of the form, not a statement from the Department. On $75,000 that gives $2,328.78 of Vermont withholding, and take-home pay of $61,186 after the lower federal tax.

What is the Vermont Child Care Contribution, and does it come out of my pay?

It is a payroll tax of 0.44% that Vermont employers pay on wages, which started on July 1, 2024. The Department of Taxes says an employer may choose to deduct and withhold up to 25 percent of it, or 0.11% of an employee’s wages, and may withhold a smaller portion or none at all. Employees have no filing requirement. This calculator does not subtract it, because nothing requires your employer to take it from you. If yours does, the most it can take on $75,000 is $82.50 a year, which would lower take-home pay from $58,907 to about $58,825. Any amount withheld is reported on your W-2.

Is there a Vermont paid family leave deduction?

We found none that is required. The state describes its paid family and medical leave program as voluntary: employers can choose to offer a plan, and the contributions can be paid by the employer, split with employees, or paid by employees as a voluntary benefit. If your employer offers a plan and deducts a premium, it will show on your pay stub, and this calculator does not include it. That is what we read on the governor’s page for the program, which carries the launch announcements from December 2022 and January 2024, not proof that nothing else exists, and your pay stub is the final word.

Do Vermont cities or towns tax my paycheck?

The Vermont withholding guide describes state withholding only, and we found no Vermont city, town or county income tax on wages in it. That is not proof that none exists. This calculator adds no local line for Vermont.

I live in Vermont and work in New Hampshire, or the reverse. Which state withholds?

The Department’s guide says Vermont withholding applies to wages paid to a Vermont resident and to a nonresident for services performed in Vermont. For a Vermont resident, the guide says that when a payment includes pay for work done outside the state, withholding is computed on the full payment and then reduced by the tax withheld for the state where the work was done. For a nonresident who works in Vermont and another state in the same pay period, the guide multiplies the Vermont tax on the full payment by the share of hours worked in Vermont. This calculator assumes you live and work in Vermont and does not model either case. New Hampshire, a neighbor whose calculator here shows no state tax on wages, has its own calculator.

My employer is in another state, but I work from home in Vermont. Does Vermont withhold?

The Department says an out-of-state employer does not have to begin withholding Vermont income tax until an employee has been working from a Vermont location for thirty days. If an employee lives and works remotely in Vermont, income earned during the whole period of living in Vermont is subject to Vermont income tax, even if the employee claims another state as home. The Department says employers should work with these employees, and the W-4VT helps them set their allowances. This calculator assumes your employer withholds.

Does a 401(k) contribution lower my Vermont withholding?

In this calculator, yes. The guide says Vermont withholding is calculated similarly to federal withholding and does not give a separate rule for 401(k) contributions, so we start from pay after a traditional 401(k) contribution, the same choice we make in most states here. On $75,000 with 6% going into a 401(k), Vermont income tax withholding falls by $297.00 a year.

How does Vermont withhold tax on a bonus?

The guide’s section on annuities, supplemental payments and deferred compensation says that for periodic payments the tax is computed using the Vermont tables, and that for non-periodic payments the Vermont withholding “can be estimated at 30% of the federal withholding.” It does not say whether a bonus falls under that estimate or goes through the tables. This calculator does not model bonuses, so ask your employer which method it uses.

Why is my Vermont paycheck different from this calculator?

Common reasons: you claimed a different number of allowances on your W-4VT, such as one for each dependent; you asked for extra withholding; health insurance premiums and other pre-tax deductions come out before tax and are not modeled here; part of your pay is a bonus; or your employer deducts something optional, such as part of the Child Care Contribution or a leave plan. If you never gave your employer a W-4VT, the guide says it may use your federal Form W-4 instead, and the Department warns that not enough tax might be withheld that way. Withholding is only an estimate of what you will owe, and the final amount is settled when you file.

How current is the Vermont formula on this page?

It comes from the Department of Taxes’ 2026 Income Tax Withholding Instructions, Tables, and Charts, document GB-1210-2026, whose cover reads “effective January 1, 2026 - expires December 31, 2026.” We read it on the Department’s own site on October 20, 2026. The W-4VT we read is the one the Department posts, and its footer reads “Rev. 12/18.” If the Department posts new tables, we update this page from them.
